How it works

Venturi meter flow rate, explained

Created by Umasankar Maity — B.Tech in Electrical Engineering, with 11+ years of industrial experience.

Reviewed by the ElectroMechCalc editorial team, comprising a qualified electrical engineer, a mechanical engineer, and a Chartered Accountant.

Last reviewed: August 2026  |  Standards referenced: ISO 5167 (venturi meter flow measurement), Bernoulli/continuity principle

A venturi meter measures flow rate by narrowing the pipe to a smaller "throat" section and measuring the pressure drop between the full-bore inlet and the throat. As the pipe narrows, the fluid must speed up to maintain the same mass flow rate (a direct consequence of the continuity equation — the same mass has to pass through a smaller area per unit time), and that increase in velocity comes at the cost of a measurable drop in pressure, following Bernoulli's principle. Measuring that pressure drop lets you work backward to calculate the flow rate that caused it.

Q = Cd × A₂ × √(2ΔP/ρ) / √(1 − β⁴)

Here, A₂ is the throat's cross-sectional area, ΔP is the measured differential pressure between inlet and throat, ρ is the fluid density, and β (beta) is the ratio of throat diameter to inlet diameter, D₂/D₁. The term (1 − β⁴) under the square root is the velocity-of-approach factor — it corrects for the fact that the fluid at the inlet is already moving, not stationary, before it reaches the throat, so the pressure drop reflects the change in velocity between the two sections rather than the throat velocity alone.

Cd, the discharge coefficient, is an empirical correction factor (typically 0.95–0.99 for a well-made classical venturi tube) that accounts for small real-world deviations from the idealized, frictionless Bernoulli assumption — primarily minor friction losses and the fact that the flow profile isn't perfectly uniform. A venturi meter's Cd is notably closer to 1.0 (i.e. closer to ideal) than most other differential-pressure meters like orifice plates, which is one reason venturi meters are favoured where measurement accuracy and low permanent pressure loss both matter.

The beta ratio itself is a key design choice: a smaller β (a more aggressive throat constriction) gives a larger, easier-to-measure ΔP for a given flow rate, improving low-flow sensitivity, but also increases permanent pressure loss and the risk of cavitation in liquids at low downstream pressure. Typical beta ratios for general service fall in the 0.4–0.75 range, balancing measurement sensitivity against pressure loss.

Worked Example

A venturi meter has a 100 mm inlet and 50 mm throat, measuring water flow with a 20 kPa differential pressure, Cd = 0.98.

  • β = 50/100 = 0.5; A₂ = π/4 × 0.05² = 0.0019635 m²
  • 1 − β⁴ = 1 − 0.5⁴ = 1 − 0.0625 = 0.9375
  • √(2×20,000/1000) = √40 = 6.3246 m/s
  • Q = 0.98 × 0.0019635 × 6.3246 / √0.9375 = 0.01217 / 0.9682 ≈ 0.01257 m³/s
  • Q ≈ 45.2 m³/hr (≈ 754 L/min)
  • Throat velocity = Q / A₂ = 0.01257 / 0.0019635 ≈ 6.40 m/s

This calculator assumes incompressible, single-phase flow with a reasonably developed velocity profile upstream; compressible (gas, high ΔP) flow and non-standard installations need compressibility and installation correction factors per ISO 5167.

Design guidance

Choosing beta ratio and discharge coefficient

ConsiderationEffect of a smaller β (more constriction)
Differential pressure signalLarger, easier to measure accurately at low flow
Permanent pressure lossHigher — more energy is not recovered downstream
Cavitation risk (liquids)Higher, since throat velocity and local pressure drop are larger
Turndown ratioGenerally better at low flow, since ΔP is a larger fraction of measurement range

The discharge coefficient Cd for a classical (Herschel-type) venturi tube manufactured to recognized standards is typically taken as 0.995 for a rough-cast inlet cone or around 0.98 for a machined one, though the exact figure should come from the meter manufacturer's calibration certificate or the applicable measurement standard rather than assumed — accurate flow measurement depends on using the correct, verified Cd for the specific meter.

Common Mistakes

Common mistakes when using a venturi flow calculation

1. Using the inlet area instead of the throat area in the flow formula. The Q formula uses A₂, the throat area — substituting the larger inlet area gives a badly wrong flow rate, since the whole point of the venturi is measuring the pressure change caused by the area change.

2. Forgetting the velocity-of-approach correction, (1 − β⁴). Skipping this term (effectively assuming β = 0, as if the inlet were infinitely large) overstates the calculated flow rate, with the error growing larger as β increases toward 1.

3. Using an assumed Cd of 1.0. A real venturi meter is not perfectly ideal — even a well-made one has a Cd slightly below 1.0. Assuming Cd = 1.0 overstates the true flow rate by a small but real margin (typically 1–5% depending on the meter).

4. Applying this incompressible-flow formula to a high-ΔP gas measurement. For gases at higher differential pressures relative to line pressure, density changes meaningfully across the venturi and a compressibility (expansibility) factor must be applied — the simple incompressible formula alone understates flow rate for compressible fluids at significant ΔP.

5. Ignoring upstream flow disturbance. Venturi meters (like most differential-pressure meters) need a sufficiently long straight, undisturbed pipe run upstream to develop a normal flow profile — elbows, valves, or other fittings too close upstream distort the flow profile and introduce measurement error not captured by this basic formula.

6. Mixing differential pressure units. ΔP is easy to receive in different units (kPa, mbar, inches of water, psi) from different instruments — converting incorrectly to the Pa this formula expects is a common source of an order-of-magnitude error.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers on beta ratio, discharge coefficient, and differential pressure flow measurement.

What is the formula for flow rate through a venturi meter?+

Q equals the discharge coefficient times the throat area times the square root of (2 times differential pressure divided by density), all divided by the square root of (1 minus beta to the fourth power), where beta is the throat-to-inlet diameter ratio. This comes from combining the continuity equation and Bernoulli's principle across the constriction.

What is the beta ratio and why does it matter?+

Beta is the ratio of throat diameter to inlet diameter (D2/D1). A smaller beta means a more aggressive constriction, which produces a larger, more easily measured differential pressure for a given flow rate, but also increases permanent pressure loss and cavitation risk in liquid service.

Why is there a velocity-of-approach correction factor in the formula?+

The fluid arriving at the venturi's throat was already moving at the inlet, not starting from rest, so the measured pressure drop reflects the change in velocity between inlet and throat rather than the throat velocity alone. The (1 minus beta to the fourth) term corrects for this, and becomes more significant as the throat gets closer in size to the inlet (higher beta).

What discharge coefficient should I use if I don't have a calibration certificate?+

For a classical venturi tube manufactured to a recognized standard, a Cd around 0.98 for a machined convergent section (or roughly 0.995 for a rough-cast one) is a reasonable estimate, but the manufacturer's actual calibrated value should be used whenever available, since Cd affects flow rate directly and proportionally.

Can this calculator be used for gas flow measurement?+

This formula assumes incompressible flow, which is a good approximation for liquids and for gases at low differential pressure relative to line pressure. For gas flow at higher differential pressures, a compressibility (expansibility) factor needs to be applied on top of this basic formula, since gas density changes measurably across the constriction.

Why does a venturi meter cause less permanent pressure loss than an orifice plate for the same flow measurement?+

The venturi's gradual, streamlined convergent and divergent sections allow much of the kinetic energy gained at the throat to be recovered as the pipe expands back to full bore, unlike an orifice plate's abrupt edges which cause more turbulent, non-recoverable energy loss. This makes venturi meters attractive where minimizing permanent pressure loss matters, despite their higher manufacturing cost.

How much straight pipe run do I need upstream of a venturi meter?+

This depends on the beta ratio and the type of upstream fitting (elbow, valve, etc.), and is specified in detail by measurement standards like ISO 5167 — typically it ranges from several to over twenty pipe diameters. Insufficient straight run before the meter distorts the velocity profile and introduces measurement error beyond what this basic formula accounts for.

What happens if the throat diameter is very close to the inlet diameter (beta close to 1)?+

As beta approaches 1, the constriction becomes very mild, producing a very small, hard-to-measure differential pressure for a given flow rate, and the velocity-of-approach correction term becomes increasingly significant and sensitive to small errors. Practical venturi meters are usually designed with beta well below 1, commonly in the 0.4 to 0.75 range, to keep the measurement both sensitive and robust.

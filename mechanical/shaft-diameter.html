How it works

How Minimum Shaft Diameter Is Calculated from Torque

Created by Umasankar Maity — B.Tech in Electrical Engineering, with 11+ years of industrial experience.

Reviewed by the ElectroMechCalc editorial team, comprising a qualified electrical engineer, a mechanical engineer, and a Chartered Accountant.

Last reviewed: August 2026  |  Standards referenced: Pure torsion shaft design (ASME/IS 210 allowable-stress approach, general machine design practice)

Shaft diameter formula (solid shaft): d = ((16 × T) ÷ (π × τ))^(1/3), where T is torque and τ is allowable shear stress. Once you know how much torque a shaft has to carry, the next question is how thick that shaft needs to be so it doesn't twist past its material's safe shear stress limit. This calculator uses the standard pure-torsion sizing formula, the same one taught in every machine design course and used as the starting point for shaft sizing in mechanical engineering practice worldwide.

Formula used: d = (16T / (π × τ_allow))^(1/3), derived from the torsion stress equation τ = T·r/J for a solid circular shaft, where J (polar moment of inertia) = πd⁴/32 and r = d/2. Substituting these into τ = Tr/J and solving for d gives the cube-root formula above, with T in N·mm and τ_allow in N/mm² (MPa) for a diameter result in millimetres.

Worked example: a shaft carrying 100 Nm (100,000 N·mm) of torque, sized against an allowable shear stress of 45 MPa for a common mild-steel shaft material, needs a minimum diameter of d = (16 × 100,000 / (π × 45))^(1/3) ≈ 28.3 mm. In practice, this would be rounded up to the next available standard stock size — typically 30 mm.

Choosing an allowable shear stress: the allowable shear stress isn't the material's raw shear yield strength — it already has a design factor of safety built in. A rough industry convention derives allowable shear stress as the material's shear yield strength divided by a chosen factor of safety (commonly 2–4 for steady, well-known loads, higher for shock or fatigue-prone applications). For plain carbon steel shafting, allowable shear stress values in the 40–60 MPa range are commonly used as a starting point, but the correct figure always depends on your specific material grade, loading type, and applicable design code.

Why this is a starting point, not the final answer: pure torsion sizing assumes the shaft only twists — no bending. Almost every real machine shaft also bends, because it usually carries something (a pulley, gear, sprocket, or coupling) that applies a radial or overhung force in addition to torque. Combined bending-and-torsion design (commonly the ASME shaft design equation, which folds bending moment and torque into a single equivalent torque with separate shock/fatigue factors for each) typically calls for a somewhat larger diameter than pure torsion alone — this calculator is the right first step, but a shaft carrying a side load needs the combined check before it's finalized.

The ASME combined-loading approach, in outline: where a shaft carries both bending moment M and torque T simultaneously (the normal case for anything with an overhung pulley, gear, or sprocket), the ASME code method computes an equivalent torque Te using both M and T with separate shock-and-fatigue factors Kb and Kt (values commonly range from 1.0 for gradually applied, steady loads up to 2.0-3.0 for loads with heavy shock). This equivalent torque then goes into the same cube-root diameter formula used here, meaning the pure-torsion result from this calculator is always a lower bound, never an overestimate, for a shaft that also experiences bending.

Solid vs hollow shafts: this calculator sizes a solid circular shaft. A hollow shaft of the same outer diameter is both lighter and, for the same outer diameter, has a slightly lower torque capacity, since some of the outer-diameter material, which contributes most to torsional strength, has been removed from the center where it contributes least. Hollow shafts are chosen specifically for weight-critical applications (aerospace, high-speed machinery) where the modest strength penalty is an acceptable trade for reduced rotating mass and inertia, and need a modified polar moment of inertia calculation rather than the solid-shaft formula used here.

Critical speed, a separate check for long slender shafts: beyond static torsion strength, a long or slender shaft has a critical (whirling) speed at which it resonates and can vibrate destructively, a function of shaft length, diameter, and support/bearing spacing entirely separate from the torque-based sizing this calculator performs. For long line shafts, high-speed spindles, or any shaft where the span between bearings is large relative to diameter, a critical speed check, keeping operating speed comfortably below the shaft's first critical speed with a margin of 20% or more, is an essential additional design step that a torsion-only diameter calculation does not cover.

Material selection and its effect on the allowable stress input: the allowable shear stress you enter is entirely dependent on the shaft material chosen, and different common shafting materials carry quite different figures. Mild steel (EN8/C45 grade, one of the most common general-purpose shafting materials) typically supports an allowable shear stress in the 40-60 MPa range with a reasonable safety factor; higher-strength alloy steels (EN24, EN19) can support meaningfully higher allowable stresses, letting a smaller diameter carry the same torque, at a higher material cost. Stainless steel shafting, chosen for corrosion resistance rather than raw strength, often has a lower allowable stress than equivalent-grade carbon steel and needs its own specific figure rather than reusing a generic "steel" assumption.

Manufacturing tolerance and fit considerations: the diameter this calculator produces is a strength-based minimum, not a manufacturing dimension with tolerances applied. In practice, a shaft diameter also needs to satisfy fit requirements with mating bearings, couplings, and seals — commonly specified using ISO fit tolerance classes (like k6 for a bearing journal, or h7/g6 for a sliding fit) — which are a separate, downstream drawing consideration once the strength-based minimum diameter from this calculator has established the size range you're working within.

Where this calculator fits in a complete shaft design workflow: a typical shaft design process starts with establishing the torque (often from a power and speed calculation, see the Shaft Torque Calculator), then uses that torque with an allowable stress to get a first-pass minimum diameter here, then layers on the combined bending-and-torsion check for any radial load, the keyway stress-concentration adjustment, a critical speed check for the specific bearing span, and finally rounds to a standard, available stock size that also satisfies bearing and coupling bore requirements. Skipping straight from this calculator's pure-torsion output to a final drawing dimension, without working through the rest of that chain, is one of the more common shortcuts that leads to an undersized shaft in service, particularly on shafts that carry an overhung pulley or gear load.

A worked comparison, torsion-only vs combined loading: consider a shaft carrying 100 Nm of torque and, additionally, a bending moment of 150 Nm from an overhung pulley load. Pure torsion sizing (this calculator) at 45 MPa allowable stress gives roughly 28.3 mm, as shown in the worked example above. Running the same shaft through the ASME combined equation with moderate shock factors typically increases the equivalent torque well above the raw 100 Nm figure, pushing the required diameter up by several millimetres, often enough to move to the next standard stock size. This is a realistic illustration of why the pure-torsion figure from this tool should be treated as a starting reference point for a shaft that also bends, not a final specification.

Summary: use this calculator to get the pure-torsion minimum shaft diameter quickly, then treat that figure as a floor rather than a ceiling — round up to a standard stock size, add a keyway allowance if the shaft carries one, run the combined bending-and-torsion check if the shaft carries a side load, and verify critical speed for any long or slender shaft before committing to a final drawing dimension.

A note on units and consistency: this calculator works internally in millimetres and MPa (N/mm²), the most common combination in Indian and international mechanical drawings and material datasheets. If your source data is in different units (inches, PSI, or SI base units like Pascals and metres), convert everything to mm and MPa before entering values, or convert the calculator's output back to your preferred units afterward, rather than mixing unit systems partway through the calculation, which is a common and easily-avoided source of order-of-magnitude errors in shaft sizing.

Finally, remember that this is a preliminary sizing tool intended to give a quick, defensible starting figure early in a design — for any shaft going into a safety-critical, high-cost, or high-volume application, the full combined-loading, fatigue, and critical-speed checks referenced throughout this page should be carried out and reviewed by a qualified mechanical engineer before the design is finalized and released for manufacture.

This preliminary-vs-final distinction is worth repeating because it's easy to treat any calculator's output as an authoritative final answer simply because it came from a formula and a computed number — but a shaft diameter, like most mechanical design outputs, is the product of several linked checks working together, not a single formula in isolation.

Worked Example

Torque = 100 Nm, allowable shear stress = 45 MPa: d = (16 × 100,000 ÷ (π × 45))1/3 ≈ 28.3 mm, rounded up to the next standard stock size of 30 mm.

This calculator sizes a solid shaft for pure torsion only — it does not account for bending moment, axial load, fatigue, or stress concentration at keyways and shoulders. For a shaft subject to combined bending and torsion (the normal case for a shaft carrying a pulley, gear, or overhung load), use a combined-loading (ASME) shaft design method instead, and apply an appropriate factor of safety. Always confirm material grade, allowable stress source, and applicable design code with a qualified engineer for any load-bearing or safety-critical shaft before manufacture.

Bar stock, ready-made shafting, and standard bearing bores are only available in discrete standard sizes — the calculated "minimum" diameter almost never lands exactly on one. Always round up (never down) to the nearest commercially available size, and double-check that size against the bore of any bearing, coupling, or pulley the shaft has to mate with, since the shaft diameter often ends up being dictated by the smallest available bearing bore rather than the bare torsion calculation once you're in the typical 20–60 mm range used in industrial drives.

It's also worth checking the calculated diameter against the shaft manufacturer's or supplier's actual stocked sizes for your chosen material grade before finalizing a drawing, since not every diameter step is necessarily stocked in every grade, and a size that requires a special mill order rather than a stock bar can meaningfully affect lead time and cost on a project even when the difference from the "ideal" rounded standard size is only a few millimetres.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Sizing a Shaft Diameter

1. Sizing for torsion only when the shaft also bends. Any shaft carrying a pulley, gear, sprocket, or overhung coupling load experiences bending in addition to torsion — pure-torsion sizing alone typically undersizes such a shaft; use a combined bending-and-torsion (ASME) method for anything carrying a side load.

2. Using shear yield strength directly instead of an allowable (design) stress. The allowable shear stress in this formula already has a safety factor built in — plugging in the raw material yield strength with no safety margin produces a diameter with essentially zero design safety factor.

3. Mixing up Nm and Nmm in the formula. The formula as commonly presented needs T in N·mm (not N·m) when τ is in N/mm² (MPa) — forgetting to multiply torque in Nm by 1000 before applying the formula understates the required diameter by roughly a factor of 10.

4. Rounding the calculated diameter down instead of up. The calculated figure is the bare minimum — rounding down to a "close enough" stock size below the calculated value removes the entire safety margin and can put the shaft below its safe torque-carrying capacity.

5. Ignoring the keyway stress concentration. Cutting a keyway into the shaft for a pulley, coupling, or gear removes material and introduces a stress riser — common practice increases the required diameter at the keyway location by roughly 6–10% (or applies a specific stress-concentration factor) rather than using the plain-shaft calculated diameter unchanged.

6. Not checking fatigue for a shaft under a cyclic or reversing load. A shaft that sees continuously varying or reversing torque (common in most rotating machinery) can fail by fatigue at a stress well below the static allowable shear stress used here — for any cyclically loaded shaft, a fatigue-based design check (endurance limit, stress concentration, surface finish factors) is required in addition to this static sizing calculation.

7. Skipping a critical speed check on a long or slender shaft. Torsion sizing alone says nothing about whether the shaft will run smoothly at operating speed — a long-span or slender shaft can be adequately strong in torsion while still being at risk of destructive resonance if operating speed approaches its critical (whirling) speed; this needs a separate dynamics check.

8. Using solid-shaft formulas for a hollow shaft, or vice versa. A hollow shaft has a different polar moment of inertia than a solid shaft of the same outer diameter — applying the solid-shaft formula to a hollow-shaft design overstates its torque capacity for a given outer diameter.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for minimum shaft diameter under pure torsion? +

d = (16T / (π × τ_allow))^(1/3), where d is the shaft diameter, T is the torque, and τ_allow is the allowable shear stress of the shaft material. Keeping T in N·mm and τ_allow in N/mm² (MPa) gives d directly in millimetres.

Where does the 16/π factor in the formula come from? +

It comes from the torsion equation τ = T·r/J, where J is the polar moment of inertia of a solid circular shaft (J = πd⁴/32) and r = d/2. Substituting and solving for d gives d³ = 16T/(πτ), hence the cube-root formula used here.

What allowable shear stress should I use for mild steel? +

A commonly used allowable shear stress for general-purpose mild steel (like EN8/C45) shafting is in the 40-60 MPa range, already incorporating a reasonable factor of safety against yield. Always check your specific material's shear yield strength and apply your own required factor of safety rather than assuming a single universal number — this varies by code, application, and material grade.

Does this calculator size for combined bending and torsion? +

No — this is a pure-torsion calculation only. Most real shafts (carrying a pulley, sprocket, or gear that applies a side load) experience combined bending and torsion simultaneously, which requires the ASME combined-shock-and-fatigue formula using an equivalent torque that folds in both bending moment and torque with their respective shock/fatigue factors — the diameter from that method is normally larger than pure-torsion sizing alone.

How do I get torque if I only know power and RPM? +

Use T (Nm) = 9550 × P(kW) ÷ N(rpm) first — our Shaft Torque Calculator does exactly this conversion — then feed the resulting torque into this shaft diameter formula.

Should I round the calculated diameter up to a standard size? +

Yes, always round up (never down) to the nearest standard/stock shaft diameter available from your supplier — common metric stock sizes step in 5mm or 10mm increments in the mid-range — since a shaft slightly larger than the bare-minimum calculated value only adds margin, while rounding down removes your safety margin entirely.

Does keyway cutting reduce the effective shaft strength? +

Yes significantly — cutting a keyway removes material and creates a stress concentration, and common design practice increases the calculated plain-shaft diameter by roughly 6–10% (or applies a specific keyway stress-concentration factor) at the keyway cross-section to compensate. This calculator does not apply that adjustment automatically.

What happens if I use too low an allowable shear stress value? +

You'll get an oversized, overly conservative shaft diameter — safe, but potentially wasteful in material, weight, and cost. Too high an allowable stress (too optimistic) is the more dangerous mistake, since it can undersize the shaft with insufficient safety margin against yielding or fatigue failure in service.

Is a hollow shaft ever stronger than a solid one for the same weight? +

Yes — for a given weight (mass) of material, a hollow shaft with a larger outer diameter and a bored-out center is torsionally stronger than a solid shaft of smaller diameter using the same mass of material, since material near the outer surface contributes far more to torsional strength than material near the center. This is why weight-critical rotating shafts (aerospace drive shafts, some high-performance machinery) are frequently designed hollow rather than solid.

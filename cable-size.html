How it works

How to Calculate Cable Size — Step by Step

Quick answer: To calculate cable size, find the load current from kW, voltage and power factor, pick the smallest cable (mm²) whose current-carrying capacity is at least that current after derating, then check voltage drop over the cable length and step up a size if the drop is too high.

  1. Find the load current (Ib). 1-phase: I = (kW × 1000) ÷ (V × PF) 3-phase: I = (kW × 1000) ÷ (√3 × V × PF) For motors: kW = (HP × 0.746) ÷ efficiency.
  2. Add a planning margin (optional). This calculator can add +20% for spare capacity. It is not a code demand or derating factor.
  3. Choose a base size from current-carrying capacity. Pick the smallest mm² size whose capacity ≥ design current, separately for copper or aluminium.
  4. Apply derating (final design). Correct the tabulated capacity for ambient temperature, grouping and installation method — see derating.
  5. Check voltage drop. 1-phase: Vd = (2 × I × L × R) ÷ 1000 3-phase: Vd = (√3 × I × L × R) ÷ 1000 If Vd% exceeds the 3% screening target, step up one size and re-check.
  6. Select protection, neutral and earth. Protective device: Ib ≤ In ≤ cable capacity. Neutral = phase size by default. Earth/CPC by rule of thumb (verify with an adiabatic check).

Worked Example: 20 kW, 3-Phase, 415 V, 60 m, Copper

  1. Load current = (20 × 1000) ÷ (1.732 × 415 × 0.85) ≈ 32.7 A.
  2. With +20% planning margin → sizing current ≈ 39.3 A.
  3. Smallest size with screening capacity ≥ 39.3 A → 10 mm² (40 A).
  4. Voltage drop at 60 m = √3 × 39.3 × 60 × 1.83 ÷ 1000 ≈ 7.5 V ≈ 1.8% → passes 3%.
  5. Result: 10 mm² copper, indicative protective device 40 A (39.3 ≤ 40 ≤ 40).

Screening result only — confirm with the applicable current-rating table for your installation method and derating.

Why Cable Size Matters

  • Heat: current through conductor resistance creates I²R heat. An undersized cable overheats, ages its insulation and can cause a fire.
  • Voltage drop: long runs lose voltage in the cable. Motors and equipment can overheat, underperform or trip on undervoltage.
  • Cost: oversizing adds material and installation cost — but a larger size may be chosen deliberately for voltage drop, derating or future load.

Ampacity

Current-Carrying Capacity & Derating Factors

Current-carrying capacity (ampacity) is the maximum continuous current a cable can carry without exceeding its insulation temperature limit. Tabulated values assume a reference condition — typically 30°C air and a single circuit. Real installations need correction:

  • Ambient temperature (Ca): hotter air → lower capacity.
  • Grouping (Cg): several loaded circuits together heat each other.
  • Thermal insulation (Ci): cables in insulated walls lose heat slowly.
  • Buried cables: soil thermal resistivity and depth also apply.
Required tabulated capacity: It ≥ In ÷ (Ca × Cg × Ci)

This size calculator does not apply these factors. Our current carrying capacity calculator does — it takes installation method (air, conduit, tray or buried), PVC 70°C / XLPE 90°C insulation, ambient temperature, grouping, thermal insulation, harmonic neutral loading and parallel runs, and returns the derated ampacity. Voltage drop

Voltage Drop Check in Cable Sizing

  • Formula: Vd% = Vd ÷ V × 100, with Vd from the 1-phase (2 × I × L × R) or 3-phase (√3 × I × L × R) formula ÷ 1000.
  • L = one-way length (m), R = conductor resistance (Ω/km), I = design current (A).
  • Screening target: 3%. Codes set their own limits — e.g. BS 7671 guidance commonly cites 3% for lighting and 5% for other uses on a public supply.
  • Limitation: resistive only (no reactance) at ~20°C. Real drop at operating temperature is higher.
  • For an R·cosφ + X·sinφ calculation, use the Voltage Drop Calculator.

Applications

Cable Sizing for Motors, Transformers & Long Runs

  • Motor cable size: enter HP with nameplate efficiency and PF. Check starting current and starting voltage drop separately. Some codes add a factor (e.g. NEC 430.22 sizes motor branch conductors at 125% of full-load current).
  • Transformer cable size: secondary current I = (kVA × 1000) ÷ (√3 × V). Enter that value in amps. Large currents often need parallel cables.
  • Long cable runs: voltage drop usually decides the size, not ampacity. Example: 5 kW single phase, 230 V, PF 0.95 → 4 mm² at 25 m, but 10 mm² at 60 m.
  • Aluminium vs copper: aluminium needs roughly one size larger for the same current and drop.
Beyond a single cable

Parallel Cables, Neutral & Earth Sizing, Laying Methods

Parallel Single-Core Cables

  • Used when one conductor per phase would be too large to pull or terminate.
  • Cable arrangement changes mutual inductance — a poor layout makes cables share current unequally.
  • Two families: trefoil (three phases grouped per circuit) and flat (evenly spaced).
  • Follow the layout diagram in the applicable standard (e.g. AS/NZS 3008.1, IEC 60364-5-52) — typically defined for up to four parallel circuits per phase.

Illustrative example layouts only, showing the general concept of grouped ("trefoil") vs flat conductor arrangement across parallel circuits — not a reproduction of any specific standard's published figure. The applicable installation standard (e.g. AS/NZS 3008.1, IEC 60364-5-52) gives the complete, specific set of recommended and alternative arrangements, including additional symmetric options and neutral-conductor placement, for the actual number of parallel conductors in your design.

Neutral Conductor Sizing

  • Default: neutral = phase conductor size (this is what the calculator shows).
  • Balanced, low-harmonic 3-phase loads: a reduced neutral may be allowed — it must still carry the out-of-balance current.
  • Many nonlinear single-phase loads (LED drivers, SMPS, IT equipment): triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th…) add in the neutral — size it equal to or larger than the phase.

Earth / CPC Conductor Sizing

  • Rule of thumb used here: same as phase up to 16 mm², 16 mm² for 16–35 mm², half the phase size above 35 mm².
  • Final design needs the adiabatic check (fault current and disconnection time) — use the Earthing Conductor Size Calculator.

Common Cable Sizing Mistakes

  • Ignoring length: a cable rated for the current can still fail the voltage-drop check.
  • Assuming PF = 1 for motors: underestimates current and undersizes cable and breaker.
  • Skipping derating: hot rooms and grouped cables reduce capacity.
  • Ignoring cores / loaded conductors: 2-, 3- and 4-core cables have different ratings (not modelled here).
  • Swapping Cu ↔ Al without re-checking the size table.

FAQ

Cable Size Calculator — Frequently Asked Questions

Content last reviewed: September 2026

How do I calculate cable size? +

Find the load current with I = (kW × 1000) ÷ (V × PF) for single phase or I = (kW × 1000) ÷ (√3 × V × PF) for three phase. Choose the smallest cable (mm²) whose current-carrying capacity, after derating, is at least that current. Then check voltage drop over the cable length and step up one size if the drop is too high.

How do I calculate cable size for a 3 phase load? +

Select Three Phase, enter the line-to-line voltage (e.g. 400 V or 415 V), load in kW, HP or A, power factor and cable length. The calculator uses I = (kW × 1000) ÷ (√3 × V × PF), picks a base size from its screening capacity table and checks voltage drop with Vd = (√3 × I × L × R) ÷ 1000.

What size cable do I need for a 10 kW three-phase load? +

At 415 V and PF 0.85, a 10 kW load draws about 16.4 A. In this calculator's screening model, that gives 4 mm² copper for a 30 m run with about 0.9% voltage drop. Longer runs, derating or a different installation method can require a larger size.

Why does cable length change the recommended size? +

Longer cables have more resistance, so voltage drop rises. For example, a 5 kW single-phase load at 230 V (PF 0.95) screens at 4 mm² copper for 25 m, but needs 10 mm² at 60 m to stay under the 3% screening target.

What is current-carrying capacity (ampacity)? +

It is the maximum continuous current a cable can carry without exceeding its insulation temperature limit — for example 70°C for PVC or 90°C for XLPE. It depends on installation method, ambient temperature and grouping, so the tabulated value must be corrected with derating factors.

Why does aluminium need a larger cable size than copper? +

Aluminium has lower conductivity than copper, so it needs a larger cross-section for the same current and voltage drop — usually about one standard size larger.

Can I use this as a UK or BS 7671 cable size calculator? +

Yes, for a preliminary size: select 230 V single phase or 400 V three phase. The result uses simplified screening values, not BS 7671 Appendix 4 tables, so confirm the final size against BS 7671 for your installation method and cable type (e.g. twin & earth or SWA).

Does this calculator cover armoured (SWA) or XLPE cables? +

Not directly — it gives one screening size per conductor material. To apply PVC 70°C or XLPE 90°C insulation, installation method, grouping and ambient derating, enter the base ampacity from your SWA/XLPE cable table in the Current Carrying Capacity Calculator.

How is the protective-device (MCB/MCCB) rating chosen? +

The calculator picks a standard rating that satisfies Ib ≤ In ≤ cable capacity. It is indicative only — final selection must also check fault level, breaking capacity, trip curve and coordination.

What size should the neutral and earth conductor be? +

Neutral defaults to the same size as the phase; increase it where many nonlinear single-phase loads cause triplen harmonics. Earth/CPC uses a rule of thumb (same as phase up to 16 mm², 16 mm² for 16–35 mm², half above 35 mm²); verify with an adiabatic calculation.

Does this replace a full cable sizing study? +

No. It is a preliminary estimate. Final design must include derating, installation method, harmonics, fault-level checks and your local wiring code, ideally reviewed by a qualified electrical engineer.

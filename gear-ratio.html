How it works

Gear Ratio Formula & Calculation

This gear ratio calculator finds the ratio between the driver and driven gears from their tooth counts, then gives output RPM, torque and power for single or multi-stage gear trains.

It also works as a gearbox calculator and gear torque calculator: add input RPM, torque and efficiency to see how a gear reduction changes speed and torque. A second mode estimates the vehicle gear ratio from engine RPM, tire size and road speed.

Gear Ratio Formula

Gear Ratio = Driven Teeth ÷ Driver Teeth Output RPM = Input RPM ÷ Gear Ratio Output Torque = Input Torque × Gear Ratio × Efficiency

A 20-tooth gear driving a 60-tooth gear gives 3:1: the output turns 3× slower with 3× more ideal torque. Meshing teeth move at the same speed where they touch, so the bigger gear must turn slower. Revolutions follow the same rule: 1800 input revolutions through 3:1 give 600 output revolutions.

Real transmitted torque is lower than ideal; how much depends on gear type, speed, load, lubrication, bearings and the number of stages.

How to Calculate Gear Ratio (Step by Step)

Example: a 1500 rpm, 5 N·m motor drives a 20T gear meshing with a 60T gear on a conveyor roller.

  1. Count the teeth. Driver (on the motor) = 20T. Driven (on the load) = 60T.
  2. Find the ratio. 60 ÷ 20 = 3:1.
  3. Find output speed. 1500 ÷ 3 = 500 rpm.
  4. Find output torque. 5 × 3 = 15 N·m (ideal, 100% efficiency).
  5. Check power. 5 N·m at 1500 rpm = 15 N·m at 500 rpm ≈ 0.785 kW. A passive gear train cannot create power; real losses reduce the output power slightly.

Gear Ratio From Diameter, Radius or Speed

Meshing gears share the same tooth size (module or diametral pitch). So pitch diameter, pitch radius and shaft speeds give the same ratio as tooth count:

Gear Ratio = Driven Pitch Diameter ÷ Driver Pitch Diameter Gear Ratio = Driven Pitch Radius ÷ Driver Pitch Radius Gear Ratio = Input Speed ÷ Output Speed Ideal Torque Ratio = Output Torque ÷ Input Torque (equals the gear ratio only with no losses)

Measure pitch diameter, not tip diameter. Tips add about one module, which skews small gears most.

Four Ways to Write a Gear Ratio

The 20T → 60T pair can be written as:

  • Fraction: 60/20, reduced to 3/1
  • Decimal: 3.0 (input turns per output turn)
  • Colon ratio: 3:1 (catalog and datasheet style)
  • Torque multiplier: ×3

Watch the notation direction. A 20T gear driving a 40T gear is 2:1 in the driven ÷ driver form used here. Some sources write the same pair as 1:2 (driver : driven), so check which way a figure is quoted.

Compound Gear Train Ratio Calculator (Multiple Gears)

A compound train stacks gear pairs on shared shafts. Multiply the stage ratios, never add them:

Overall Ratio = Stage 1 × Stage 2 × … × Stage n

Example (4 gears, 2 stages): 1800 rpm, 2 N·m input.

  1. Stage 1: 15T drives 45T → 45 ÷ 15 = 3.
  2. Stage 2: a 20T gear on the 45T shaft drives 60T → 60 ÷ 20 = 3.
  3. Overall: 3 × 3 = 9:1.
  4. Output: 1800 ÷ 9 = 200 rpm, 2 × 9 = 18 N·m.

Two moderate-ratio stages can be more practical than one very high single-stage reduction, depending on gear geometry, centre distance, strength and allowable size. In the calculator, press "+ Add stage" for up to four stages.

Gear Torque Calculator: Torque From Gear Ratio

Output Torque = Input Torque × Gear Ratio × Efficiency

Example: 5 N·m through a 3:1 pair at 95% efficiency gives 5 × 3 × 0.95 = 14.25 N·m (15 N·m ideal).

  1. Get the overall ratio (single pair or compound product).
  2. Multiply: Output torque = Input torque × Ratio.
  3. Apply efficiency: multiply by the manufacturer's rated gearbox efficiency. It varies with gear type, size, speed, lubrication, load and number of stages. To get torque from shaft power and speed, use the Shaft Torque Calculator.

The tooth-count ratio sets the ideal (kinematic) speed relationship. Efficiency is applied to the transmitted torque and power. This calculator does not model tooth deflection, backlash, bearing losses or load-dependent gearbox behaviour.

Gearbox Calculator: Ratio, RPM and Torque

A gearbox (gear reducer) is a housed gear train. Its ratio equals input speed ÷ output speed, or the product of its stage ratios.

Gearbox Ratio = Input RPM ÷ Output RPM = Stage 1 × Stage 2 × … Output RPM = Motor RPM ÷ Gearbox Ratio Output Torque = Motor Torque × Gearbox Ratio × Efficiency

Used as a gear reduction calculator, a ratio above 1 lowers output speed and raises available output torque, subject to efficiency and other losses. For a catalog gearbox with a known ratio such as 5:1, enter any tooth pair with the same ratio (for example 20T and 100T) plus the gearbox's rated efficiency.

Hunting Tooth Effect and Gear-Tooth Wear

The tooth-pair repeat period is N₂ ÷ gcd(N₁, N₂) driver revolutions: the number of driver turns before the same driver tooth meets the same driven tooth again.

  • 20T → 60T: gcd = 20, so 60 ÷ 20 = 3 driver revolutions. Tooth pairs repeat quickly, and a local tooth defect keeps loading the same few spots.
  • 17T → 60T: gcd = 1, so 60 ÷ 1 = 60 driver revolutions. This "hunting tooth" pair spreads repeated contact over many more revolutions.

Actual wear still depends on load, lubrication, alignment and tooth geometry. The calculator shows this repeat period for every stage.

Common Gear Ratio Mistakes

  • Swapping driver and driven. Always driven ÷ driver, or a reduction becomes an overdrive.
  • Ignoring efficiency. 100% is ideal. Real output torque is lower, especially over many stages.
  • Using one stage's ratio for a multi-stage gearbox. Multiply all stages.
  • Counting an idler as a stage. An idler only changes direction, not the ratio.
  • Mixing modules or pitches. The diameter form only works if both gears share one module (or diametral pitch).
  • Treating any tooth count as buildable. Very small pinions (roughly below 17 teeth on a standard 20° profile) may need profile shift to avoid undercut. The ratio alone does not check geometry or strength.
  • Mixing up the cycling convention. Cyclists quote chainring ÷ cog, the inverse of driven ÷ driver.

Mechanical advantage

Speed or Torque: What a Gear Ratio Trades

The speed-for-torque trade in numbers, for a 10T gear driving a 40T gear:

  1. Ratio: 40 ÷ 10 = 4:1. The small gear turns 4 times per turn of the big gear.
  2. Speed: 1200 rpm in → 300 rpm out.
  3. Torque: 10 N·m in → 40 N·m ideal. Actual transmitted torque is lower after losses: about 38.8 N·m at 97% efficiency.

Exactly 1:1Direct drive: speed and torque unchangedDirect-drive gear in many manual gearboxes
Less than 1 (e.g. 0.25:1)Overdrive: faster output, less torqueHand-cranked drill, car overdrive gears, a bicycle's top gear

The hand-cranked drill is the classic speed example: a large crown wheel on the handle turns a small pinion on the chuck, so one slow turn of your wrist spins the bit several times. The bicycle is the classic torque example: shifting to a big rear cog on a hill means more pedal turns per wheel turn, but each push needs far less force. Both machines follow the same rule, just in opposite directions.

Idler gears

Idler Gears: Same Ratio, Different Direction

Every time two external gears mesh, the direction of rotation flips. Put a third gear in the middle and the output flips back, turning the same way as the input. That middle gear is an idler, and its tooth count has no effect on the overall ratio. The algebra shows why: with a driver (N1), idler (Ni) and driven gear (N2), the ratio is (Ni ÷ N1) × (N2 ÷ Ni) = N2 ÷ N1. The idler's teeth cancel out completely.

Toggle the demo below. The 20-tooth input and 30-tooth output keep the same 1.5:1 ratio in both cases; only the output's direction changes.

The rule generalises to any chain of external gears: count the meshes. An odd number of meshes reverses the output; an even number keeps it turning the same way. The calculator applies this rule using the number of stages plus the number of idlers you enter. A few related cases are worth knowing:

  • Reverse gear in a manual car gearbox usually works by sliding a small idler into the gear path, which is why reverse can sound different (it often uses straight-cut teeth) and why the car's wheels turn backward with the engine still turning the same way.
  • Internal (ring) gears don't reverse direction. A pinion meshing inside a ring gear turns the same way as the ring, which is one reason planetary sets behave differently from simple gear pairs.
  • Open belts and chains keep the direction; a crossed belt reverses it. A bicycle chain turns the rear wheel the same way as the pedals for this reason.
  • Idlers also bridge distance. When two shafts are too far apart to mesh directly, an idler fills the gap without forcing either gear to become oversized.
Vehicles

Vehicle Gear Ratio & Tire Size: Car and Motorcycle Examples

In a vehicle the "gear ratio" that matters for speed is the overall ratio: how many times the engine crankshaft turns for each turn of the driven wheel. It is the product of every reduction between the crank and the tire, and it links engine RPM to road speed through the tire's circumference. The calculator's second mode solves any one of the four quantities from the other three.

Overall Ratio = Gearbox Ratio × Final Drive Ratio (× Primary Ratio on most motorcycles) Wheel RPM = Engine RPM ÷ Overall Ratio Vehicle Speed (m/min) = Wheel RPM × π × Tire Diameter (m) Vehicle Speed (km/h) = Wheel RPM × π × Tire Diameter (m) × 60 ÷ 1000 Tire Diameter (mm) = 2 × Width × Aspect Ratio ÷ 100 + Rim Diameter (in) × 25.4

Everything here assumes no slip: no clutch slip, no torque-converter slip in an automatic, and no tire slip against the road. A loaded tire also rolls on a slightly smaller effective radius than its nominal size, so treat results as close estimates, not as a calibrated speedometer reading.

4-Wheeler Example: a Front-Wheel-Drive Hatchback

Take an illustrative five-speed hatchback with a 4.07:1 final drive on 185/65 R15 tires. The tire diameter is 2 × 185 × 0.65 + 15 × 25.4 = 240.5 + 381 = 621.5 mm, so each wheel turn covers π × 0.6215 ≈ 1.953 m. In 5th gear (0.85:1) the overall ratio is 0.85 × 4.07 = 3.4595:1. At 2500 rpm the wheels turn 2500 ÷ 3.4595 ≈ 722.6 rpm, and the car travels 722.6 × 1.953 × 60 ÷ 1000 ≈ 84.7 km/h (about 52.6 mph). Press "Car (4-wheeler)" in the calculator to load these numbers.

The pattern is what matters. 1st gear multiplies engine torque more than 14 times at the wheels (ideal, before losses), which is what gets a loaded car moving from rest, but it tops out at low road speed. 5th gear multiplies torque only about 3.5 times, so the engine turns slowly at highway speed, which cuts noise and fuel use. 4th gear here is close to 1:1, and 5th is below 1, an overdrive. Changing tire size shifts every row: a larger-diameter tire raises road speed at the same rpm while reducing effective wheel torque, just like a numerically lower final drive would.

2-Wheeler Example: a Chain-Drive Motorcycle

Most chain-drive motorcycles have three reductions in series instead of a car's two. A primary reduction (gears or a short chain) links the crankshaft to the clutch and gearbox, the gearbox supplies the selected ratio, and the final drive is the chain and sprockets. With an illustrative 3.35:1 primary, a 0.92:1 top gear and 14/42 sprockets (42 ÷ 14 = 3:1), the overall ratio is 3.35 × 0.92 × 3 = 9.246:1. On a 100/80-17 rear tire (2 × 100 × 0.80 + 17 × 25.4 = 591.8 mm), 7000 rpm gives a wheel speed of 7000 ÷ 9.246 ≈ 757.1 rpm and a road speed of about 84.5 km/h (52.5 mph). Sprocket swaps are the cheapest ratio change on a motorcycle. Going from a 14-tooth to a 15-tooth front sprocket drops the final drive from 3.0 to 42 ÷ 15 = 2.8, so the overall top-gear ratio falls to 8.63:1 and road speed at 7000 rpm rises by about 7% to roughly 90.5 km/h. The trade is about 7% less pulling force at the rear wheel in every gear. Adding teeth at the rear does the opposite. Either change also affects chain length, so check the chain and adjuster range before fitting a different sprocket.

Scooters and bicycles work differently. Most scooters use a CVT belt drive whose ratio changes continuously with speed, so there is no fixed gear table; the vehicle mode still gives the instantaneous overall ratio if you know engine speed and road speed at that moment. Bicycles are human-powered 2-wheelers with the same chain-and-sprocket math, but riders usually quote chainring ÷ cog (for example 50 ÷ 25 = 2.0 wheel turns per crank turn), which is the inverse of the driven ÷ driver convention used everywhere else on this page.

Gear & Drive Types

Common Gear & Drive Types

A gear is a toothed wheel that passes rotation, torque and speed from one shaft to another by positive engagement: the teeth lock into each other. A set of gears working together is a gear train. The first gear to receive power (from a motor, an engine, or a hand crank) is the driver, the last one is the driven or output gear, and the way their shafts are arranged decides which type of gear you need.

Spur gears

Straight teeth on parallel shafts. Simple, efficient and cheap; the output turns the opposite way.

Ratio = Z₂ ÷ Z₁

Helical gears

Angled teeth engage gradually, so they run quieter and carry more load, at the cost of axial thrust on the bearings.

Ratio = Z₂ ÷ Z₁

Bevel gears

Cone-shaped gears that turn the drive through an angle, usually 90°. The heart of a car's differential.

Ratio = Z₂ ÷ Z₁

Worm drive

A screw-like worm turns a wheel at 90°. Huge reductions in one stage. Some worm drives resist back-driving, but self-locking depends on lead angle, friction and operating conditions; never assume a worm gearbox is automatically self-locking.

Ratio = Wheel teeth ÷ Worm starts

Rack & pinion

A gear running on a straight toothed bar turns rotation into straight-line motion, as in car steering.

Travel per turn = π × pitch dia.

Planetary set

A sun, several planets and a ring share one axis. Compact, high torque, used in automatic gearboxes and gearmotors.

Ring fixed, sun in, carrier out: Ratio = 1 + Z_ring ÷ Z_sun

Gear pairs provide positive tooth engagement with a fixed kinematic ratio, unlike belt drives that can slip under some conditions, and are the usual choice wherever the shafts are close together and precise, backlash-controlled motion matters. Chain and belt drives let the two shafts sit farther apart without an intermediate gear train — chains handle higher torque with minimal slip, while belts run quieter and tolerate minor misalignment but can slip under sudden overload unless properly tensioned.

A worked worm example: a 40-tooth wheel driven by a single-start worm gives 40:1 in one stage; a two-start worm on the same wheel gives 20:1. A worked planetary example: with a 12-tooth sun as input, a 36-tooth ring held still and the planet carrier as output, the ratio is 1 + 36 ÷ 12 = 4:1, and the carrier turns in the same direction as the sun. This formula applies only to that arrangement; holding the sun or the carrier instead gives a different ratio.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate gear ratio? +

Divide the driven (output) gear's teeth by the driver (input) gear's teeth: Gear Ratio = Driven Teeth ÷ Driver Teeth. A 20-tooth driver and a 60-tooth driven gear give 60 ÷ 20 = 3:1. Then Output RPM = Input RPM ÷ Gear Ratio, and ideal Output Torque = Input Torque × Gear Ratio. For several gear pairs in series, multiply the stage ratios.

What does a gear ratio actually tell you? +

It tells you how speed and torque change as power passes from one gear to the next. A ratio greater than 1 (e.g. 3:1) means the output shaft turns slower but with more torque; a ratio less than 1 means the output turns faster but with less torque.

Why does output speed go down when torque goes up? +

Meshing gears must move at the same linear speed at their point of contact, so a larger driven gear rotates proportionally slower than the smaller driver gear. Power is the product of speed and torque, and an ideal gear train conserves mechanical power, so that drop in angular speed comes with a proportional rise in torque. In a real gear train, friction and other losses reduce the output power and therefore the available output torque.

Do I need both RPM and torque to get a gear ratio? +

No. In gear-teeth mode the gear ratio only needs the driver and driven teeth counts for each stage. Input speed, input torque and efficiency are optional — fill in either or both if you also want the output speed, output torque and output power.

Does this calculation apply to chain and belt drives too? +

Yes — the same ratio math applies to sprocket-and-chain drives (using tooth counts) and to pulley-and-belt drives (using pulley diameters in place of teeth), since both are governed by the same principle of matching linear speed at the point of contact.

Are these results accurate enough for professional use? +

This calculator is suitable for preliminary calculations and educational use. It uses standard gear and vehicle-speed equations with ideal (loss-free, no-slip) behaviour unless you enter an efficiency below 100%. It does not verify gear strength, tooth bending stress, contact stress, shaft strength, bearing capacity, thermal limits, backlash or manufacturer-specific gearbox performance. For equipment selection or safety-critical applications, verify against manufacturer data and applicable engineering standards.

How do I calculate torque from gear ratio? +

Output Torque = Input Torque × Gear Ratio, using Gear Ratio = Driven Teeth ÷ Driver Teeth (or the overall ratio for a compound gear train). For example, 5 N·m of input torque through a 3:1 gear ratio gives an ideal output torque of 5 × 3 = 15 N·m. This is the ideal, loss-free figure — real gearboxes deliver somewhat less due to mechanical losses. Actual efficiency varies significantly with gear type, size, speed, lubrication, load and number of stages, so use the manufacturer's rated efficiency when available. Enter the efficiency in the calculator to include that loss.

How do I calculate gear ratio for a gear train with multiple gears? +

For a compound gear train with several meshing pairs in series, calculate each individual stage's ratio (driven teeth ÷ driver teeth) separately, then multiply all the stage ratios together to get the overall gear ratio — the ratios multiply, they don't add. For example, two stages each with a 3:1 ratio give an overall ratio of 3 × 3 = 9:1, not 3 + 3 = 6:1. In this calculator, press “+ Add stage” for each extra pair (up to four) and it multiplies them for you.

Does a harmonic drive use the same gear ratio formula? +

No — a harmonic (strain wave) drive uses a fundamentally different mechanism from simple meshing spur or bevel gears, so it doesn't follow the driven-teeth-over-driver-teeth formula this calculator uses. Its ratio instead depends on the small tooth-count difference between its flexspline and circular spline components, which lets harmonic drives reach very high reduction ratios in a compact, low-backlash package; the exact ratio depends on that tooth-count difference and the specific configuration. This calculator is built for conventional gear pairs and gear trains, not harmonic drive kinematics — consult the specific harmonic drive manufacturer's technical documentation for that calculation.

What does an idler gear do to the gear ratio? +

Nothing — an idler between the driver and driven gear does not change the overall ratio, whatever its tooth count, because its teeth cancel out of the calculation. It changes the direction of rotation instead: every external gear mesh reverses direction, so adding one idler makes the output turn the same way as the input. Idlers are also used to bridge a gap between shafts that are too far apart to mesh directly, and a sliding idler is how many manual gearboxes produce reverse gear.

How do you calculate gearbox ratio from input and output speed? +

Divide input speed by output speed: Gear Ratio = Input Speed ÷ Output Speed, with both in the same unit. For example, a motor at 1450 rpm driving an output shaft at 290 rpm has a 1450 ÷ 290 = 5:1 ratio. This is useful on site, where a tachometer reading is easier than opening a gearbox to count teeth.

How do I calculate gear ratio from engine RPM, tire size and vehicle speed? +

First find wheel speed: Wheel RPM = Vehicle speed in metres per minute ÷ (π × Tire diameter in metres). Then Overall gear ratio = Engine RPM ÷ Wheel RPM. For example, 3000 rpm at 100 km/h on a 631.9 mm (205/55 R16) tire gives a wheel speed of about 839.6 rpm and an overall ratio of about 3.57:1. This is the combined gearbox × final-drive ratio and assumes no clutch or tire slip.

What is the difference between gearbox ratio and final drive ratio? +

The gearbox (transmission) ratio changes with each selected gear, while the final drive ratio is the fixed reduction in the differential on most cars, or the chain and sprockets on most motorcycles. The overall ratio the engine works through is the product of the two (and of the primary reduction on a motorcycle). Divide the overall ratio by the final drive ratio to get the transmission-side ratio for the gear you are in: the gearbox ratio on a car, or primary × gearbox on most motorcycles. The calculator does this when you fill in the final drive field.

How do I get tire diameter from a tire size like 205/55 R16? +

Tire diameter (mm) = 2 × section width × aspect ratio ÷ 100 + rim diameter × 25.4. For 205/55 R16: 2 × 205 × 0.55 + 16 × 25.4 = 225.5 + 406.4 = 631.9 mm. The calculator's tire-size field does this for common metric car and motorcycle sizes (such as 100/80-17) and inch sizes (such as 31x10.50R15); sizes without an aspect ratio need the diameter entered directly. The real rolling diameter under load is usually a little smaller than this nominal figure.

Can a gear ratio be less than 1? +

Yes. A ratio below 1 (for example 0.8:1) means the driven gear has fewer teeth than the driver, so the output turns faster than the input with proportionally less torque. Car overdrive gears and hand-cranked drills use this to raise speed.

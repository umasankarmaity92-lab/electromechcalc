How it works

How This Engineering Unit Converter Works

Engineering work often mixes unit systems: metric drawings with imperial nameplates, bar gauges with psi-rated valves, or kW loads with BTU ratings. This tool brings the nine conversions used most in design and maintenance work into one place: length, area, weight, pressure, temperature, energy, flow rate, frequency and speed.

How to use it: choose a converter, enter a value and select the From and To units. The result updates as you type or when you press Calculate. Each result shows the conversion factor, the same value in every other unit of that category, and a swap button to reverse the direction.

How the conversion works: most categories convert through one base unit with a fixed factor, such as meters for length or pascals for pressure. Temperature is the exception, because Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin have different zero points, so it uses offset formulas instead of a single factor.

Typical uses: converting pump flow from US GPM to m³/h, checking a bar reading against a psi rating, relating motor speed in RPM to supply frequency in Hz, and converting inch dimensions to millimeters before machining.

Length Converter: mm, cm, m, km, inch, foot, yard and mile

Base unit: meter (m)1 in = 25.4 mm (exact)

Use the length converter to switch between metric units (mm, cm, m, km) and imperial units (inch, foot, yard, mile) on drawings, datasheets and site measurements. The underlying unit definitions are exact, so repeated conversions do not drift; values shown with ≈ are rounded for readability.

Exact length conversion factors

UnitIn metersDefinition
1 inch (in)0.0254 mExactly 25.4 mm
1 foot (ft)0.3048 m12 inches
1 yard (yd)0.9144 m3 feet
1 mile (mi)1,609.344 m5,280 feet
1 meter (m)1 m≈ 3.28084 ft

How to convert length

Multiply by the factor of the unit you have, then divide by the factor of the unit you want. Example: 12 in × 25.4 = 304.8 mm, and 100 m ÷ 0.3048 = 328.084 ft.

Where length conversion is used

  • Drawings and fabrication: checking metric drawing dimensions against inch callouts on imported machinery, fasteners and pipe fittings.
  • Cable and conduit takeoffs: reconciling run lengths quoted in meters and feet in the same bill of quantities.
  • Route and site surveys: converting miles to kilometers for transmission line, pipeline or road distances.
  • Tolerances: reading thousandths of an inch (mils) correctly against a metric micrometer.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Wrong direction: multiplying when you should divide gives an inverted result. The factor line under the result always shows “1 unit = X unit”.
  • Using length factors for area: area factors are squared, so use the Area converter instead of applying 0.3048 to ft².

How to convert area

Multiply by the square-meter factor of the starting unit and divide by the factor of the target unit. Example: a 5,000 ft² roof × 0.09290304 = 464.515 m².

Where area conversion is used

  • Land and site planning: moving between acres or hectares in land records and m² or ft² in layouts.
  • Solar projects: checking usable roof or ground area against panel counts and array layouts.
  • Sheet metal and insulation: comparing ft² quantities on imported datasheets with metric fabrication drawings.
  • Conductor cross-sections: keeping cable and busbar areas consistently in mm².

Mistakes to avoid

  • Applying a length factor: 1 m = 3.28 ft, but 1 m² = 10.76 ft², not 3.28 ft².
  • Mixing acre and hectare: a hectare is about 2.47 times larger than an acre, a large error over a whole site.

How to convert pressure

Multiply by the pascal factor of the starting unit and divide by the target factor. Example: 150 psi × 6,894.757 ÷ 100,000 = 10.342 bar.

Where pressure conversion is used

  • Compressed air and hydraulics: matching psi-rated cylinders and valves to a system set in bar.
  • Boilers and vessels: comparing gauge readings in kgf/cm² with design pressures in kPa or bar.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Safety-critical values: for relief valve set points and pressure vessel documents, confirm the final value against the applicable code.

Temperature Converter: Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin

°F = °C × 9/5 + 32K = °C + 273.15

Temperature conversion uses offset formulas, not a single factor, because Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin start from different zero points. The converter goes through Celsius internally and applies the full formula in both directions.

Temperature conversion formulas

From → ToFormulaExample
°C → °F°F = °C × 9/5 + 3240 °C = 104 °F
°F → °C°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9212 °F = 100 °C
°C → KK = °C + 273.1525 °C = 298.15 K
K → °C°C = K − 273.150 K = −273.15 °C
Absolute zero0 K−459.67 °F

Temperature values vs temperature differences

A reading needs the full formula, but a change in temperature only uses the scale factor: a rise of 10 °C (10 K) equals a rise of 18 °F, not 50 °F. Use this converter for readings, and multiply differences by 1.8 (°C to °F) yourself.

Where temperature conversion is used

  • Motors and insulation: comparing °F limits on imported datasheets with °C insulation classes under IEC 60034.
  • HVAC and chillers: moving setpoints between °F equipment and a °C building management system.
  • Cable derating: keeping ambient and conductor temperatures in °C before applying derating factors.
  • Science and lighting: Kelvin for thermodynamic formulas and LED color temperature.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating it like a ratio: doubling °C does not double °F.
  • Converting a rise with the offset: adding 32 to a temperature difference gives a badly wrong result.

How to convert energy

Multiply by the joule factor of the starting unit and divide by the target factor. Example: 12,000 BTU = 3.517 kWh of energy. As a rate, 12,000 BTU/h corresponds to about 3.517 kW of cooling capacity.

Where energy conversion is used

  • Electricity and solar: daily yield, bills and load profiles in kWh.
  • Batteries and UPS: Ah × V gives Wh, which can then be compared with load energy in kWh.
  • HVAC: comparing BTU cooling ratings with kW-based heat loads.
  • Thermal calculations: converting kcal or BTU data into kJ for process heat balances.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Cooling vs electrical input: 3.517 kW of cooling is not 3.517 kW of electricity; input power depends on the unit's EER or COP.
  • Calorie variants: this tool uses the 4.184 J thermochemical calorie. The International Table calorie is 4.1868 J, a 0.07% difference.

Frequency formulas

  • Period: f = 1 ÷ T. A 50 Hz waveform has a period of 0.02 s (20 ms).
  • Waves: f = v ÷ λ, where v is wave velocity and λ is wavelength.
  • Rotation: 1 Hz = 60 RPM = 2π rad/s = 360 °/s, for one cycle per revolution.
  • Synchronous speed: Ns = 120 × f ÷ P. A 4-pole machine runs at 1,500 RPM on 50 Hz and 1,800 RPM on 60 Hz; induction motors run slightly slower because of slip.

Unit converter vs electrical frequency converter

  • This tool: changes how a frequency is written, such as Hz to RPM.
  • Static frequency converter: power equipment with a rectifier (AC to DC) and inverter (DC to new AC frequency).
  • Rotary frequency converter: a motor-generator set; the generator's speed and poles set the output frequency, and its rotating mass rides through very short disturbances.
  • Special frequencies: 400 Hz aircraft ground power, 16.7 Hz rail traction in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Sweden and Norway, and 25 Hz on parts of the US Northeast Corridor.

Running equipment on the other frequency

  • Electronics: many chargers and switch-mode supplies are rated “50/60 Hz”; check the nameplate.
  • 50 Hz motor on 60 Hz: about 20% faster at the same voltage, so fan and pump loads can draw much more power.
  • 60 Hz motor or transformer on 50 Hz: higher magnetic flux, magnetizing current and heating unless voltage is reduced in the same ratio.

  • Conveyors and fluid velocity: comparing m/s design values with ft/s ratings on imported equipment.

  • Radius instead of diameter: using radius in π × D × N ÷ 60 halves the tip speed and hides an overspeed risk.
FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is temperature conversion different from the other categories? +

Length, weight, pressure, and similar units all scale from zero — doubling a length in meters doubles it in feet too. Temperature scales don't share a common zero point (0°C is not 0°F), so converting between Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin requires an offset-based formula rather than a simple multiplication factor.

How accurate are the conversion factors? +

The factors come from standard SI and internationally recognized unit definitions (as maintained under NIST and BIPM conventions), and many are exact by definition, such as 1 inch = 25.4 mm and 1 bar = 100,000 Pa. Results are calculated with full floating-point precision and rounded for display, so use the conversion factor shown for higher-precision work. For legally mandated, metrology-grade or safety-critical values, cross-check against the authoritative standard for your jurisdiction.

How do I convert bar to psi? +

Multiply bar by 14.5038 to get psi, or multiply psi by 0.0689476 to get bar. For example, 10 bar = 145.04 psi. A pressure gauge in kgf/cm² converts with 1 kgf/cm² = 0.980665 bar = 14.2233 psi.

How do I convert GPM to m³/h? +

Multiply US gallons per minute by 0.227125 to get m³/h, or multiply m³/h by 4.40287 to get US GPM. For example, 250 US GPM = 56.78 m³/h. An imperial gallon is about 20% larger, so confirm which gallon a datasheet uses.

How do I convert Hz to RPM or rad/s? +

Multiply hertz by 60 to get RPM and by 2π (about 6.283185) to get radians per second; to go back, divide RPM by 60 or rad/s by 2π. For example, 50 Hz = 3,000 RPM = 314.159 rad/s. RPM and rad/s are rotational speed units, linked to hertz here on the basis of one cycle per revolution, which is why they sit in the Frequency & Rotational Speed converter. For motor or generator speed, use N = 120 × f ÷ P, where P is the number of poles.

Is this the same as an electrical frequency converter? +

No. This tool converts how a frequency value is expressed, such as Hz to RPM or kHz to Hz. An electrical frequency converter (frequency changer) is power equipment that changes the actual supply frequency, for example 50 Hz to 60 Hz, using either a static rectifier-inverter design or a rotary motor-generator set.

Does the Flow Rate converter handle both volume and mass flow? +

This converter covers volumetric flow rate units only (m³/s, m³/h, L/s, L/min, US GPM, and CFM). Mass flow rate (kg/s, lb/h, etc.) depends on fluid density and isn't a fixed unit conversion, so it isn't included here.

Is this engineering unit converter free to use? +

Yes — all nine converters in this tool are completely free with no sign-up, exactly like every other calculator on ElectroMechCalc.

Can I use this converter for engineering calculations, not just quick reference? +

Yes, it's built for exactly that — engineers commonly use it to cross-check datasheets from different regions (metric vs imperial), convert nameplate values between unit systems, and verify unit consistency before plugging numbers into a formula. Just confirm the specific precision you need matches the tool's decimal display for critical calculations.

Why do I need nine different converter categories instead of one generic tool? +

Different physical quantities (length, pressure, temperature, energy, etc.) have entirely different unit systems and conversion factors that aren't interchangeable — a single generic converter would be error-prone and confusing. Splitting by category keeps each conversion focused and reduces the chance of selecting an incompatible unit pairing.

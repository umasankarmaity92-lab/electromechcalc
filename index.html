06 — FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is kVA, and how is it different from kW? +

kVA (kilovolt-amperes) is apparent power — the total power flowing in an AC circuit, including both the power doing useful work and the power lost to reactive loads like motor coils. kW (kilowatts) is real power, the portion that actually performs work. The two are related by power factor: kW = kVA × PF. A power factor of 1.0 means all apparent power is real power; most industrial loads run between 0.8 and 0.95.

Why is Power Factor important when sizing equipment? +

Power factor determines how much of the delivered apparent power (kVA) is actually converted into usable work (kW). A low power factor means transformers, generators, and cables must be rated larger to deliver the same real power, raising equipment cost and line losses. Many utilities also penalize industrial customers for poor power factor, which is why correction capacitors (APFC panels) are common on factory floors.

How do I choose the right transformer or DG size for my load? +

Add up your total connected load in kW, divide by the expected power factor to get kVA, then add a safety margin — typically 20–25% — for future load growth and motor starting surges. Our Transformer Size and DG Size calculators handle this automatically and round up to the nearest standard rating.

Are these calculators accurate enough for professional use? +

Yes — every formula used is the same standard formula taught in electrical and mechanical engineering coursework and referenced in field handbooks. That said, for safety-critical or code-compliance decisions, always verify results against your local electrical code and have them reviewed by a licensed engineer. The EPF, SIP, and CAGR calculators follow the standard methods used by EPFO and major mutual fund houses, and are for educational planning purposes only — they do not constitute financial advice.

Is this aligned to Indian usage? +

Yes — voltage defaults (230V single-phase, 415V three-phase), cable sizing references, and standard equipment ratings follow IS/BIS conventions used in India, and the financial calculators (EPF, PPF, gratuity, income tax) follow Indian rules and EPFO methods, with results shown in ₹.

Can I use this on-site? +

Yes — every calculator is mobile-friendly, loads fast on limited data, and needs no login or account, so you can use it standing next to a panel or a pump skid, not just at a desk.

Why ElectroMechCalc Exists, and How to Get the Most Out of It

Reviewed under our Editorial Policy

A Toolkit Built Around One Idea: Show the Formula

Almost every electrical, mechanical, or financial calculation you'll ever need on the job or in coursework comes down to a handful of well-established formulas. The hard part usually isn't the math itself — it's remembering the right constant, tracking down the correct standard, or doing the arithmetic quickly enough to keep moving. ElectroMechCalc was built to remove that friction. Every calculator on this site — whether it's sizing a transformer, converting kVA to kW, estimating a motor's full-load current, or projecting an EPF maturity value — shows the underlying formula alongside the result, so the number you get is something you can verify, learn from, and trust, not a black box you have to take on faith.

Three Categories, One Consistent Standard

The site is organized into three broad categories: electrical calculators, mechanical calculators, and financial planning calculators. The electrical section covers the calculations that come up most often around panels, transformers, cables, protection devices, and motors — transformer kVA sizing, kVA-to-kW and kW-to-current conversions, motor full-load and starting current, cable ampacity and voltage drop, fuse/MCB/MCCB/MPCB sizing, contactor and relay settings, CT/PT ratios, load factor and demand, power quality (voltage unbalance, harmonic distortion), APFC capacitor sizing for power factor correction, DG and generator fuel consumption, inverter and UPS sizing, battery charging time, solar panel sizing and tilt angle, and lighting design. The mechanical section handles the calculations technicians, maintenance engineers, and machine designers reach for on rotating equipment, fluid systems, and structural elements — bearing L10 life, gear ratios, pump head and total dynamic head, belt length and belt speed, pulley ratios, cooling tower efficiency, shaft torque and diameter, beam deflection and bending stress, bolt torque, spring rate, flywheel kinetic energy, and roller chain length. The financial section applies the same formula-first philosophy to personal finance — EPF, gratuity, SIP and lump-sum investment growth, CAGR, income tax comparison between the old and new regimes, fixed and recurring deposits, PPF, and NPS. Different domains, same principle: show the math, use standard-aligned defaults, and give an answer fast enough to be useful in the middle of a task.

Who Actually Uses These Tools

Most visitors fall into one of a few groups. Electrical and mechanical engineering students use the calculators to check homework and lab results against a known-good formula rather than trusting a single worked example from a textbook. Site electricians and technicians use them standing in front of a panel or a pump skid, where a fast, mobile-friendly calculator beats flipping through a handbook or hunting for a signal to load a heavier tool. Plant maintenance teams use the bearing life, pump head, and cooling tower calculators to plan replacements and diagnose underperforming equipment before it fails outright. Facility managers use the transformer, DG, UPS, and solar sizing calculators when planning capacity for a new load, an expansion, or a backup power system. And because a lot of that same audience also plans their own retirement and investments, we added a small, focused set of financial calculators — EPF, SIP, CAGR, and the rest — built with the same emphasis on transparent, checkable math.

How to Use a Calculator on This Site

Every calculator follows the same basic pattern. You enter the inputs the formula actually needs — nothing extra, no forced sign-up, no account. The result appears instantly in the browser, along with the formula that produced it and a short explanation of what each term means. If a calculator involves an industry-standard constant or default assumption — a typical power factor, a standard safety margin, a common motor efficiency — that default is disclosed rather than buried, and in most cases you can override it if your specific situation calls for something different. Because the page shows its work, you can sanity-check the result by hand if you want to, use it to double-check a manual calculation, or use it to learn the formula itself rather than just the answer.

Our electrical calculators are built around formulas and default values referenced against recognized standards bodies — IEC for transformer and cable sizing, IEEE for motor and power system calculations, BIS for India-specific equipment ratings, and NEC for cable ampacity and installation practice where applicable. Our financial calculators are cross-checked against the published methods used by EPFO and major mutual fund houses, so the maturity and growth figures line up with how those institutions actually calculate them. That said, this site is an educational and planning resource, not a substitute for professional engineering judgment or licensed financial advice. For anything safety-critical, code-compliance related, or involving a significant financial decision, treat the calculator output as a strong starting estimate and verify it against your local electrical code or a qualified professional before acting on it.

How it works

Understanding Education Loan EMI

An education loan (called a student loan in some countries) is disbursed in stages during your course, and repayment (EMI) typically starts only after a moratorium or deferment period. The exact rule varies by country and lender, but it generally covers the study period plus a grace period — for example, India's IBA model education loan scheme uses the course period plus one year, or 6-12 months after landing a job, whichever is earlier. During the moratorium, full EMI repayment is generally deferred, although some lenders may require or allow interest servicing; interest itself usually still accrues either way.

Formula used: Capitalized Principal = P + (P × annual rate ÷ 100 × moratorium months ÷ 12), where the accrued simple interest during moratorium is added to the principal. EMI is then calculated on this capitalized principal using EMI = P′ × r × (1+r)ⁿ ÷ ((1+r)ⁿ − 1) over the repayment tenure.

Worked example: a loan of 800,000 (in any currency — for example, ₹8,00,000 for a student loan in India) at 10.5% p.a. with a 30-month moratorium accrues about 210,000 in interest, capitalizing the principal to roughly 1,010,000. Repaid over 8 years (96 months), this gives an EMI of approximately 15,594.

Why this differs from other EMI calculators: the moratorium period means your effective loan amount at the start of repayment is higher than what was originally disbursed, since unpaid interest is capitalized into the principal at the end of the moratorium — this calculator accounts for that capitalization before computing your EMI.

This calculator is for educational planning purposes only. Actual EMI may vary depending on your lender's specific moratorium/deferment and capitalization policy; confirm the precise figure with your bank, NBFC, or loan servicer.

Modeling assumption: this calculator assumes the full loan amount is disbursed at the start of the moratorium and accrues interest for the entire moratorium period entered. In practice, many lenders disburse education loan funds in stages across the course (e.g., per semester or year), so the actual interest accrued — and therefore the actual capitalized principal and EMI — may be lower than this calculator shows if your disbursement is staged rather than upfront.

Official sources for the tax and scheme references on this page: India's Income Tax Department (incometaxindia.gov.in) for Section 80E; the US Internal Revenue Service (irs.gov) for the Student Loan Interest Deduction; and the Indian Banks' Association (iba.org.in) for the IBA model education loan scheme. Tax and scheme rules change over time — always confirm current figures directly with these sources or a qualified professional.

If you (or your family) can afford to pay the interest as it accrues during the moratorium — even in small amounts — the principal stays at the originally disbursed amount instead of growing through capitalization, which meaningfully lowers your EMI once regular repayment begins. Some lenders may also offer an interest-rate concession for borrowers who service interest during the moratorium; check your specific loan agreement for whether this applies.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Planning an Education Loan

1. Ignoring interest accrual during the moratorium. Many students assume "no EMI" means "no cost" during the course — but interest typically still accrues and capitalizes, quietly inflating the loan you'll eventually repay.

2. Not exploring your country's tax benefit for education loan interest. This varies significantly by country — in India, Section 80E (see Income Tax Department) allows the entire interest paid to be deducted from taxable income for up to 8 years; in the US, the Student Loan Interest Deduction (see IRS) allows up to $2,500 per year, subject to an income phase-out. Check what applies where you file taxes and factor it into your real cost-of-borrowing comparison.

3. Borrowing the maximum sanctioned amount instead of the actual requirement. Loan amounts are often sanctioned generously to cover contingencies — draw down only what's needed each semester to minimize interest accrual on undisbursed funds.

4. Not checking collateral and margin money requirements. Many lenders require collateral or a co-applicant above certain loan thresholds, and margin money (a portion of costs to be self-funded) is common too — the specific thresholds and requirements vary significantly by country, scheme, and lender, so check your lender's current policy before applying.

5. Overlooking employment/repayment risk after the course. Since regular EMIs may start before securing stable employment, keep a buffer or consider partial interest servicing during the course to avoid repayment stress.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a moratorium period in an education loan? +

The moratorium/deferment period varies by country and lender, but it generally covers the study period plus a grace period during which the borrower isn't required to pay EMIs, though interest typically continues to accrue. For example, India's IBA model education loan scheme uses a repayment holiday of the course period plus one year, or 6-12 months after securing a job, whichever is earlier — exact rules elsewhere differ, so confirm your own lender's terms.

Does interest accrue during the moratorium period? +

Yes, for most education loans, simple interest accrues on the disbursed amount during the moratorium period. This accrued interest is usually capitalized (added to the principal) at the end of the moratorium, and EMI is then calculated on this higher principal.

Is it beneficial to pay interest during the moratorium period? +

Yes, paying at least the accruing interest during the moratorium (called 'simple interest servicing') prevents it from being capitalized into the principal, which reduces your total EMI and total interest once repayment begins. Some lenders may also offer an interest-rate concession for this — check your lender's current terms.

What is the tax benefit on education loan interest? +

This varies significantly by country, and tax rules change over time, so treat the figures below as a starting point, not current legal advice. In India, Section 80E of the Income Tax Act allows the entire interest paid on an education loan to be deducted from taxable income, with no upper limit, for up to 8 years from the start of repayment (principal is not deductible) — confirm current limits with the Income Tax Department (incometaxindia.gov.in). In the US, the Student Loan Interest Deduction allows up to $2,500 per year, subject to an income phase-out — confirm current limits with the IRS (irs.gov). Always check your own country's tax authority for the rules and limits that currently apply to you.

What is the maximum repayment tenure for an education loan? +

This depends on the lender and country. In India, education loan repayment tenure typically ranges from 5 to 15 years after the moratorium period ends, depending on the loan amount and lender policy. Tenures and moratorium/deferment rules elsewhere (e.g., US federal or private student loans, UK income-contingent repayment) follow different structures, so confirm the specific terms with your lender.

Can I prepay or foreclose an education loan early? +

Many lenders permit prepayment or early repayment, but fees, lock-in periods, and eligibility vary significantly by lender and country. Early repayment can reduce future interest, but compare any prepayment charges, tax effects, and alternative uses of your cash before deciding — always check your specific loan agreement for prepayment terms and any applicable fee.

What if I don't secure a job before the moratorium ends? +

The moratorium normally follows the lender's contractual schedule rather than automatically extending until you find employment — typically the course duration plus a grace period, though this varies by lender and country. If you haven't secured stable income by the time it ends, EMIs generally still begin; some lenders offer a temporary extension or restructuring option in genuine hardship cases, but this isn't guaranteed and should be discussed directly with your lender before the moratorium ends.

Is the interest rate on an education loan fixed or floating? +

Both fixed and floating-rate education loans exist; availability and prevalence vary by country and lender. A floating rate moves with a reference benchmark (such as a central bank rate or an internal lending rate) and can change your EMI over the loan term, while a fixed rate stays constant. Compare the total cost under both if your lender offers a choice.

What documents or eligibility are typically required for an education loan? +

Requirements vary by lender and country, but commonly include admission proof from a recognized institution, academic records, identity/address proof, and income proof for a co-applicant or guarantor (especially for loans requiring collateral). Some lenders also require the co-applicant to have a stable income or credit history. Check your specific lender's checklist before applying, since documentation requirements differ significantly across countries.

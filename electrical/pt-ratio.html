How it works

How PT Ratio Is Calculated and Selected

PT ratio example: an 11,000 V primary system with a standard 110 V secondary gives a PT ratio of 11000:110, or 100:1. A potential transformer (PT), also called a voltage transformer (VT), scales a high system voltage down to a small, standardized secondary voltage that meters, relays, and instruments are built to accept — conceptually similar to how a CT scales current, but working on voltage instead, and connected in parallel across the voltage being measured rather than in series with current flow.

Formula used: PT Ratio = Primary (System) Voltage ÷ Secondary Voltage. Primary voltage is the actual system nominal voltage (11kV, 33kV, 66kV, and so on); secondary voltage follows a standard value, most commonly 110V internationally.

Worked example: an 11,000V (11kV) system uses a PT with a 110V secondary standard. PT Ratio = 11,000 ÷ 110 = 100, expressed as 11000:110 or simplified to 100:1. For a 33kV system with the same 110V secondary standard, PT Ratio = 33,000 ÷ 110 = 300:1.

Why PT primary voltage doesn't get a sizing margin the way CT current rating does: PT primary voltage should be selected to match the actual system voltage and the applicable equipment/insulation class it's designed for — there's no equivalent to "oversizing for future load growth" the way there is with CT current rating, since a PT rated for a specific voltage class has insulation and turns ratio engineered for that class, not a margin above it. Ratio alone does not fully specify a PT: an 11kV system calls for an 11kV-class PT, and using a PT rated for a different, incompatible voltage class (even one seemingly "close" like 6.6kV or 22kV) isn't a matter of accuracy trade-offs the way CT margin selection is — it's a mismatch against the equipment's actual voltage and insulation rating.

Line-to-line versus line-to-neutral PT connection: for a three-phase system, PTs can be connected either line-to-line (measuring the full voltage between two phases, with a 110V line-to-line secondary) or line-to-neutral/phase-to-ground (measuring a single phase's voltage relative to neutral or ground, typically with a secondary of 110÷√3 ≈ 63.5V for a system whose line-to-line secondary would be 110V). Both configurations serve different metering and protection purposes — three-phase power metering typically needs line-to-line (or a full three-phase PT set) measurement, while some protection functions (like earth fault or unbalance detection) specifically need phase-to-neutral or phase-to-ground voltage information that a line-to-line-only connection wouldn't provide.

Standard PT primary voltages follow the system voltage series: unlike CT primary ratings (which follow their own dedicated standard current series), PT primary voltage ratings simply match the standard nominal system voltage series used across power distribution and transmission — 415V, 3.3kV, 6.6kV, 11kV, 22kV, 33kV, 66kV, 132kV, 220kV, and so on, varying somewhat by country and utility practice. A PT is manufactured for a specific system voltage class (or sometimes a small family of closely related classes), with insulation, creepage distance, and turns ratio all engineered around that specific voltage — selecting the correct voltage class PT for your actual system is a straightforward, near-exact match exercise, unlike CT current rating selection which involves the accuracy-versus-future-growth trade-off discussed for CTs.

Why insulation level matters as much as ratio for PT selection: beyond the turns ratio calculated here, a PT's insulation must be rated for the actual system voltage class it's connected to, including margin for transient overvoltages the system might experience (switching surges, lightning-related transients on outdoor systems). This insulation rating is a separate specification from the voltage ratio itself — two PTs might have an identical 100:1 ratio but be rated for different maximum system voltages and insulation levels, so ratio alone doesn't fully specify a PT; insulation class must also match the actual installation's voltage class and environment.

Three-phase PT configurations: for full three-phase voltage measurement, installations commonly use either three single-phase PTs (one per phase, connected phase-to-neutral, giving complete individual phase voltage information), a single three-phase PT unit (integrating three windings in one housing, more compact but sometimes less flexible), or the open-delta (V-connection) arrangement using just two PTs where a neutral reference isn't practically available. The right configuration depends on what voltage information is actually needed (just line-to-line magnitudes, or full per-phase detail for unbalance/earth-fault detection) and practical installation constraints like available space and whether a system neutral point exists to reference against.

Summary: use PT Ratio = System Primary Voltage ÷ Standard Secondary Voltage, select a PT/VT whose rated primary voltage and insulation/equipment ratings are appropriate for the system voltage and installation requirements (not a margin above it, unlike CT current sizing), confirm both ratio and insulation level match your installation, and remember the critical safety distinction: never short-circuit an energized PT secondary.

Connecting PT selection to overall metering and protection design: PT ratio, alongside CT ratio, feeds directly into how meters and relays interpret raw secondary readings back into actual primary system values — a power meter reading PT and CT secondary voltage and current needs both ratios correctly configured to calculate accurate real-world power, energy, and other derived quantities. Getting PT ratio right is therefore just as fundamental to accurate metering as CT ratio, even though the specific selection considerations (fixed voltage class match vs current-based utilization optimization) differ meaningfully between the two instrument transformer types.

PT selection in medium and high voltage switchgear: in medium-voltage systems (commonly covering roughly the 1–35/36kV range, though the exact boundary varies by applicable standard or regional classification) and high-voltage switchgear — often referred to as HT (High Tension) switchgear in Indian and subcontinent practice, as distinct from LT (Low Tension) — PTs are often a specifically ordered, factory-fitted component of the switchgear assembly rather than a separately field-installed device, given the insulation coordination and safety clearances required at these voltage levels. Understanding the ratio and configuration principles covered here remains useful for specifying requirements to a switchgear manufacturer, verifying delivered equipment matches specification, or troubleshooting an existing installation, even when the PT itself isn't something typically installed as a standalone field retrofit at these voltage classes.

Whether you're specifying a new switchgear order, verifying an existing installation's documentation, or simply building foundational understanding of how voltage measurement scales down safely from high system voltages to instrument-friendly levels, the same ratio principle covered here underlies every one of these tasks.

Worked Example

11,000V system, 110V secondary: PT Ratio = 11,000 ÷ 110 = 100:1. For 33,000V system: PT Ratio = 33,000 ÷ 110 = 300:1.

This calculator gives PT ratio from primary and secondary voltage — actual PT selection also requires checking accuracy class, rated burden (VA), insulation level appropriate for the system voltage, and whether a single-phase or three-phase (or open-delta) PT configuration is needed. Always confirm final PT selection against the manufacturer's technical data and the applicable standard for your specific metering or protection application. Remember the critical safety distinction from CTs: never short-circuit an energized PT secondary, and always verify insulation level matches your actual system voltage class before installation.

Field Reference

PT Terminals, Connection & Measuring Procedure

Terminal markings: common IEC-style markings use P1/P2 for a single-phase PT's primary terminals and 1a/1n (sometimes shown as a/n) for its secondary terminals, with a polarity dot marking corresponding instantaneous polarity between primary and secondary. A common ANSI/IEEE-style equivalent uses H1/H2 for the primary and X1/X2 for the secondary. Actual designations can vary by manufacturer and configuration, so always confirm against the specific PT's nameplate and drawing. Correct polarity matters for any power or power-factor measurement, where reversed secondary polarity can invert the sign of the reading.

Simplified single-line reference only — not a substitute for the manufacturer's wiring diagram or your site's approved drawing. A line-to-line PT can be connected across any two suitable points on the bus per the actual design. Markings shown follow common IEC-style convention (P1/P2 primary, 1a/1n secondary, dot = polarity; a common ANSI-style equivalent is H1/H2 primary, X1/X2 secondary) — confirm against your specific PT's nameplate.

Typical connection: the primary (P1/P2) connects directly across the two points on the bus being measured (line-to-line), or across one phase and neutral/ground for a phase-to-neutral connection — commonly through a primary-side fuse or current-limiting device on each lead, protecting the bus from a PT winding fault. The secondary (1a/1n) feeds the connected voltmeter, energy meter, and/or protection relay — normally in parallel, since each burden device shares the same secondary voltage. A secondary MCB or fuse is commonly fitted to protect the wiring run. A single designated secondary grounding (earthing) point is a common practice where required by the protection/installation design and applicable site standard — confirm the actual grounding arrangement against your site's approved drawing rather than assuming it.

Field measuring / verification procedure:

  1. Confirm the PT's nameplate ratio, accuracy class, rated burden, and voltage factor before energizing — this calculator's ratio result should match the nameplate value.
  2. Isolate and lock out the primary circuit if any primary-side work is needed; on live-system verification, only the secondary side is normally accessed, with appropriate PPE and site permit for working near an energized system.
  3. Visually verify terminal markings and polarity dots against the wiring/single-line diagram before connecting any test instrument.
  4. Measure secondary voltage (1a to 1n) with a calibrated voltmeter while the system is at a known primary voltage — use an approved/known primary voltage reference from the test procedure, existing metering, or a properly rated measurement system; do not directly measure medium/high voltage yourself unless you are specifically authorized and equipped for that task. Then compute the measured ratio = primary voltage ÷ measured secondary voltage.
  5. Compare the measured ratio against the nameplate ratio and this calculator's result — a significant deviation may indicate a wiring, connection, or PT fault and should be investigated before relying on the PT for metering or protection.
  6. Record the verification result, date, and instrument used as part of the site's commissioning or maintenance documentation.

⚠️ PT BACK-FEED HAZARD Never apply a test voltage to the PT secondary while the primary is de-energized or floating without specific engineering authorization — this can back-feed the primary side through the PT's turns ratio, producing a hazardous high voltage at the primary terminals. Follow your site's approved PT testing procedure and never short-circuit an energized PT secondary.

These two instrument transformer types are often discussed together since they serve complementary roles (getting both voltage and current down to safe, standardized levels for the same metering or protection scheme), but their opposite safety practices for open versus shorted secondaries are worth committing to memory precisely because they're opposite &mdash.

This reversal of safety practice between the two device types is a common point of confusion for anyone newer to instrument transformers — a useful memory aid is that a CT is fundamentally a current source (so it needs somewhere for current to flow, meaning open-circuit is dangerous and short-circuit is safe), while a PT is fundamentally a voltage source (so a short circuit forces high current, meaning short-circuit is dangerous and open-circuit is safe). Keeping this underlying source-type distinction in mind, rather than memorizing the rules by rote, makes the correct practice easier to recall correctly under real field conditions.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Calculating PT Ratio

1. Applying CT-style current margin thinking to PT voltage rating selection. PT primary voltage is fixed by the actual system voltage class, not selected with an accuracy-driven margin the way CT current rating is — there's no equivalent "utilization percentage" consideration for PT primary voltage selection.

2. Confusing which secondary voltage standard (110V, 100V, or 120V) applies to your specific installation. Using the wrong standard secondary voltage assumption in a ratio calculation gives an incorrect ratio — confirm the actual standard used by your region, utility, or specific meter/relay manufacturer before calculating.

3. Applying CT secondary safety practice (safe to short) to a PT. This is a genuinely dangerous mistake — a PT secondary should never be short-circuited while energized, the opposite safety rule from CT secondaries, since a PT behaves like a voltage source and a short circuit drives high, damaging current.

4. Not distinguishing line-to-line from line-to-neutral PT secondary voltage when checking meter compatibility. A meter expecting a 110V line-to-line input won't read correctly if fed a 63.5V line-to-neutral PT secondary output, or vice versa — confirm which connection type both the PT and the connected instrument actually use.

5. Ignoring PT burden compatibility when connecting multiple meters or relays. Like CT burden, connecting devices whose combined VA burden exceeds the PT's rated burden can degrade accuracy below its nameplate specification — check total connected burden, not just ratio selection alone.

6. Assuming a single PT automatically provides adequate accuracy for both metering and protection without checking accuracy class. While PTs are less prone to the dramatic metering-vs-protection accuracy conflict that CTs experience at fault current levels, critical or high-voltage applications may still specifically require a protection-class (3P/6P) PT rather than assuming a general metering-class unit is adequate for both purposes.

7. Overlooking insulation level as a separate specification from ratio. Two PTs can share an identical voltage ratio while being rated for different maximum system voltages and insulation levels — confirm insulation class matches your actual system voltage and environment, not just the calculated ratio number.

8. Choosing PT configuration (three single-phase, one three-phase unit, or open-delta) without considering what protection functions actually need. Some protection functions specifically require full per-phase voltage information that an open-delta or line-to-line-only configuration doesn't provide — confirm your configuration choice supports every metering and protection function the installation actually needs, not just line-to-line magnitude measurement.

9. Ignoring terminal polarity when connecting for power or power-factor measurement. Reversing the secondary polarity relative to the primary (mixing up 1a and 1n, or P1 and P2) doesn't change the ratio, but it can invert the sign of a power or power-factor reading — always verify polarity dot markings against the wiring diagram, not just the ratio, before commissioning any metering or protection scheme.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for PT ratio? +

PT Ratio = Primary (System) Voltage ÷ Secondary Voltage. For a three-phase system, primary voltage is typically the line-to-line system voltage, and secondary voltage follows a standard value (commonly 110V internationally, sometimes 100V or 120V depending on region and standard).

What is the standard PT secondary voltage? +

Common VT secondary ratings include 100V, 110V, and 120V depending on the applicable standard, region, and equipment specification — 110V is widely used under IEC-influenced practice, 120V is common in North American practice, and 100V is used in some countries, but always confirm which standard your specific meters, relays, and regional practice actually use before assuming a value.

How is PT different from CT in terms of what it measures? +

A CT (current transformer) scales down current, has a primary that carries the actual circuit current in series with the load, and its secondary is essentially treated as a current source. A PT (potential transformer, also called a voltage transformer or VT) scales down voltage, has a primary connected in parallel across the voltage being measured, and its secondary behaves like a voltage source — the two devices work on fundamentally different principles despite both being types of instrument transformers.

Why should PT primary voltage be selected for the actual system voltage? +

Unlike CT sizing (which benefits from being closely matched to actual load current for accuracy reasons), PT primary voltage rating doesn't get an accuracy-driven margin the way CT current rating does — PT/VT selection should account for the system's nominal voltage, the applicable voltage class, insulation requirements, grounding arrangement, and manufacturer specification, since the PT's primary winding insulation and turns ratio are engineered for a specific voltage class, not a margin above it. Ratio alone does not define the correct transformer for a given system.

What accuracy classes are used for PTs, similar to CT accuracy classes? +

PT/VT accuracy classes are specified according to the applicable instrument-transformer standard and intended duty — common metering classes include 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0, prioritizing accuracy at normal system voltage for revenue metering, while common protection classes include 3P and 6P designations (depending on the applicable standard), prioritizing reasonable accuracy during the voltage dips associated with fault conditions, which matters for protection relays that need accurate voltage information even during a disturbance.

Can the same PT serve both metering and protection functions? +

Often yes, more commonly than with CTs, since voltage measurement during normal conditions and during most fault conditions doesn't diverge as dramatically as current measurement does (where fault current can be many multiples of normal load current) — but dedicated protection-class PTs or separate windings are still used in some critical or high-voltage applications where the specific accuracy-during-disturbance requirement matters most.

What is an open-delta PT connection and when is it used? +

An open-delta (or V-connection) arrangement uses two single-phase PTs, connected across two of the three line-to-line pairs, to derive the three-phase line-to-line voltage relationships of a three-phase system, rather than using three separate PTs — this reduces cost and is common in three-wire (no neutral available) medium-voltage systems. The exact quantities available depend on the specific connection and system arrangement, and this configuration does not provide independent phase-to-neutral voltages the way three individual PTs would.

Does PT burden matter the same way CT burden does? +

Yes, conceptually — PT burden is the total VA load presented by connected meters, relays, and wiring on the PT secondary, and like CT burden, connecting a burden significantly different from (especially exceeding) the PT's rated burden can degrade its actual accuracy below its nameplate specification — always check total connected burden against the PT's rated burden capacity.

Is a PT secondary circuit dangerous to leave open, the way an open CT secondary is? +

No, the opposite concern applies — a PT's secondary should never be short-circuited while energized (since it behaves like a voltage source, a short circuit would drive very high, damaging current). A PT/VT secondary is normally not subject to the open-circuit hazard associated with a CT, but it must still be treated as an energized circuit at its rated secondary voltage. This is exactly the reverse safety consideration from a CT, where the secondary should never be left open but is safe to short-circuit, so it's worth being clear on which instrument transformer you're working with before applying either safety practice.

What do the PT terminal markings P1/P2 and 1a/1n mean? +

Common IEC-style markings use P1/P2 for the primary winding terminals and 1a/1n (or a/n) for the secondary winding terminals, with a polarity dot indicating corresponding instantaneous polarity between primary and secondary — a common ANSI/IEEE-style equivalent uses H1/H2 for the primary and X1/X2 for the secondary. Always confirm actual terminal designations against the specific PT's nameplate and manufacturer drawing, since designations can vary by manufacturer and configuration. Correct polarity connection matters for any application involving power or power-factor measurement, where reversed secondary polarity can invert the sign of the reading.

What is PT rated voltage factor (Vf) and why does it matter for protection? +

Rated voltage factor (Vf) specifies the multiple of rated primary voltage a PT must accurately withstand, and for how long — common IEC 61869-3 values are 1.2 continuous, 1.5 for 30 seconds, or 1.9 for 8 hours. This matters most on non-effectively earthed systems (isolated or resonant/Petersen-coil earthed), where a single line-to-earth fault can raise the healthy phases' voltage toward line value for an extended period before clearing — a PT with only Vf 1.2 continuous may not maintain accuracy or withstand that condition safely, so a higher Vf (typically 1.9 for 8 hours) is generally selected for protection duty on these systems.

What is HT PT ratio, and does this calculator cover HT applications? +

HT (High Tension) is common Indian and subcontinent electrical engineering terminology for medium- and high-voltage systems, typically above 1kV, as distinct from LT (Low Tension) for low-voltage systems below that. An "HT PT ratio" simply refers to the PT ratio calculation for a potential transformer connected to an HT system, such as an 11kV or 33kV HT PT — this calculator's Primary Voltage field works for both HT and LT systems, so just enter your actual system primary voltage to get the HT PT ratio for your installation.

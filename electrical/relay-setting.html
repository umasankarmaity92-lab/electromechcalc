How to Determine IDMT Relay Pickup and TMS Settings

How a relay setting is worked out: pickup current, plug setting and TMS. Already have a configured pickup and TMS and only need the trip time? Use the TOC/IDMT Trip Time Calculator.

1. Determine the Maximum Load Current

The highest current the circuit carries continuously, on the CT primary side.

  • Feeder: maximum expected demand, including any permitted overload
  • Transformer or motor circuit: usually based on the equipment rated current
  • Enter it as Maximum Load Current

2. Select the Relay Pickup Current

Formula: Pickup Current = Maximum Load Current × Design Margin

  • Pickup must be above maximum load, so the relay ignores normal operation
  • Pickup must be below the minimum fault current the relay has to detect
  • A margin of 1.25–1.3× is a common starting value for a quick estimate, not a universal rule
  • Final margin depends on motor starting/inrush, transformer energization, permitted overload, relay/CT tolerance and coordination
  • Optional Minimum Fault Current input: the calculator flags a pickup at or above it (preliminary check only)

3. Convert the Pickup to a Plug Setting (%)

Formula: Plug Setting (%) = (Pickup Current ÷ CT Primary Rating) × 100

Example: 375 A on a 400/5 A CT → 93.75% → about 4.69 A at the relay.

  • Relay rated current = CT secondary (1 A or 5 A)
  • Discrete taps (for example 50–200%): the calculator selects the next higher tap, so margin over load is not lost. This is a simplified method, not a universal rule
  • Numerical relays: choose Continuous if pickup can be set finely
  • Always confirm the settings your relay actually offers

4. Calculate the Plug Setting Multiplier (PSM)

Formula: PSM = Fault Current ÷ Pickup Current

  • Shows how many times above pickup the relay sees the design fault
  • Sets the operating point on the selected curve
  • Use the actual selected pickup (tap value), not the theoretical one
  • PSM theory and trip times: see the TOC/IDMT Trip Time Calculator

5. Select the IDMT Curve

  • Standard Inverse (SI): general-purpose choice, and the curve this calculator solves for
  • Very Inverse (VI) / Extremely Inverse (EI): steeper, for specific grading needs (see curve guide below)
  • The curve must match the one configured in the relay
  • The same target time needs a different TMS on each curve

6. Determine the Required TMS

Formula (IEC SI): TMS = Desired Operating Time ÷ [0.14 ÷ (PSM0.02 − 1)]

  • Desired time comes from coordination: downstream clearing time plus the coordination time interval
  • TMS scales the delay without changing the curve shape
  • The calculator shows the theoretical TMS and the recalculated TMS at the actual tap; use the recalculated one

7. Verify Practical Relay Settings

  • Check the relay’s pickup and TMS increments
  • For discrete taps, recalculate PSM and TMS at the actual pickup
  • Nearby TMS steps (illustrative 0.05): the lower step trips faster, the higher step adds coordination margin
  • Numerical relays often allow finer or continuous settings; verify the resolution and configured curve

8. Check Protection Coordination

  • Two relays in series with identical settings would operate together
  • The upstream relay gets a longer operating time for the same fault, usually through a higher TMS
  • Keep a Coordination Time Interval (CTI) between adjacent devices (0.2–0.4 s is a commonly cited illustrative range)
  • CTI must cover breaker clearing time, relay overshoot and CT/relay tolerance
  • Work from the most remote downstream relay back toward the source
  • This calculator sets one relay only; multi-relay grading is a separate study

9. Validate the Final Settings

  • Check against the relay manufacturer’s documentation
  • Check against the fault-current study, using both maximum and minimum fault current
  • A complete coordination study is needed before applying settings to an installation
  • To check the operating time at other fault currents or on VI/EI/IEEE curves, use the TOC/IDMT Trip Time Calculator

Choosing the IDMT Curve Type for Relay Settings

Curve Type Typical use in setting design Effect on the setting
Standard Inverse (SI)General-purpose feeder and transformer overcurrent protection; the curve this calculator solves forModerate change of time with current, so TMS and pickup do most of the grading
Very Inverse (VI)Feeders where fault current falls substantially with distance from the source, so successive relays see clearly different fault levelsTime drops steeply as current rises, giving more grading margin between relays at different locations
Extremely Inverse (EI)Coordinating with fuses, or on circuits with inrush or cold-load pickupSteepest of the three; very long times just above pickup and very short times at high current
  • These are typical guidelines, not fixed rules; follow the coordination study and utility practice
  • Each curve has its own constants, so the same target time needs a different TMS on each
  • Never enter a TMS calculated for SI into a relay set to VI, EI or an IEEE curve
  • IEEE/ANSI relays use a Time Dial (TD), not TMS, and the two are not interchangeable; follow the relay manual
  • Constants, equations, IEEE curves and a side-by-side comparison: TOC/IDMT Trip Time Calculator
Distribution System Context

LV Release Setting vs HV/MV Relay Setting

The calculator above sets one IEC Standard Inverse relay. In a real distribution system that relay is one link in a chain from the HV/MV incomer to the LV loads, and each link is set with a different philosophy.

LV release setting for end equipment (motors, capacitor banks)

  • Sits at the end of the chain, with no downstream breaker to coordinate with, so the instantaneous element may trip with minimal intentional delay (subject to trip-unit limits, starting/inrush and coordination)
  • FLA (Full Load Ampere): motor rated running current, used for the thermal/overload element
  • LRA (Locked Rotor Ampere): the nameplate locked-rotor current, a starting reference but not the same as the actual starting current. Actual starting current, its asymmetrical (DC-offset) peak and acceleration time depend on the starter type, supply voltage and load. Set the instantaneous pickup above the expected starting current with margin, and verify it against the motor starting profile and the relay/trip-unit characteristic
  • Capacitor bank energizing inrush plays the same role as LRA
  • −ve tolerance: add margin for the breaker trip unit and CT tolerance, so the release does not trip early

LV release setting for a transformer's LV main (incomer)

  • Every downstream feeder passes through it, so it must never trip first for a fault a downstream feeder should clear
  • Instantaneous protection may need restraint or different coordination; the setting depends on the breaker, fault level and selectivity needs
  • Aim: the main’s curve sits above every downstream feeder curve at any realistic current
  • Approach A – known fixed loads: base pickup on the combined demand of the known feeders (added FLAs plus margin). Tight match, but revisit when a feeder is added
  • Approach B – transformer rating: a preliminary starting point: transformer full-load current with a design margin (1.25–1.5× is only an illustrative range, not a rule). Stays valid as feeders change, but is less tightly matched. Check permitted overload and thermal limits, energization inrush and downstream coordination before adopting any value
  • Bus coupler: set with reference to the more heavily loaded bus, and grade its curve between the feeder curves and the transformer main curves
  • Ground fault: set from the earthing arrangement, the available earth-fault level and the ground-fault sensing/CT arrangement. It is often more sensitive than the phase element, but there is no universal ratio, so verify it against the breaker trip-unit and coordination data

HV/MV relay setting concepts

  • The relay reads CT current and trips a separate breaker
  • IDMT: operating time falls automatically as fault current rises (the family this calculator’s curve belongs to)
  • DMT: a fixed delay once current exceeds pickup, which gives simpler time-graded coordination
  • Motor instantaneous (50): above starting current with margin, but low enough to clear a terminal or winding fault quickly
  • Transformer instantaneous (50): the A/B split above is only a starting scale. Also account for energization inrush, transformer impedance and through-fault withstand, CT characteristics, differential protection, downstream coordination and breaker interrupting capability
  • Through-fault current: fault current flowing through a transformer for an external fault (typically on the LV side). The transformer must withstand it until downstream protection clears the fault
  • Earth fault: senses residual (zero-sequence) current via a core-balance CT or the residual connection of three phase CTs, and its setting depends on the neutral grounding method, the available earth-fault level, CT arrangement and standing unbalance. It is often more sensitive than the phase element, but there is no universal ratio
  • RMU: fault current paths depend on the ring configuration and operating state. Base settings on the relevant supply paths, not on one assumed direction
  • Several transformers on a common HV bus: there is no single generic formula. The current seen by each relay depends on the network configuration, fault location, relay location and operating state (for example, bus-coupler open or closed). Calculate the currents for each relevant fault case, then set and coordinate each relay with its own upstream and downstream devices

Coordination discrimination: LV versus HV/MV

  • LV: check manufacturer time-current curves together with selectivity data (tolerances, instantaneous/short-time overrides, published selectivity tables). Many LV devices are graded by comparing curves and tolerances rather than by a fixed time step, and where instantaneous or short-time elements overlap, the manufacturer’s selectivity (discrimination) tables or tested data decide the outcome
  • HV/MV: both time and current discrimination matter. A deliberate Coordination Time Interval (CTI), commonly in the few-hundred-millisecond range (IEEE 242, the “Buff Book”, is a widely referenced source), absorbs breaker interrupting time, relay overshoot and CT/relay tolerance
  • None of this changes the single-relay calculation on this page; check the result against the actual settings of every adjacent device before finalizing
Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Calculating Relay Settings

1. Pickup too close to maximum load. Too little margin causes nuisance tripping; too much reduces sensitivity. 1.25–1.3× is a starting point, not a fixed rule.

2. Mixing CT primary and secondary values. Load and fault currents are usually primary amps, while the plug setting refers to the relay’s rated (secondary) current. Keep track of the side at every step.

3. Using a TMS calculated for a different curve. A TMS worked out for SI is wrong on a relay set to VI, EI or an IEEE curve (which uses Time Dial). Confirm the configured curve first.

4. Not checking coordination with adjacent devices. A relay that is correct in isolation can still fail to grade with the devices upstream and downstream.

5. Using an unverified fault current. PSM and TMS depend entirely on it. Take it from a fault-current study, not an assumption.

6. Assuming the exact TMS can be set. Real relays offer discrete steps. Rounding down trips faster, rounding up adds margin; re-verify the operating time of the step you choose.

7. Rounding the pickup tap down without recalculating. It shrinks the margin over load, and any tap change alters PSM and TMS. Select the tap first, then recalculate.

8. Checking only maximum fault current. A pickup that clears the load can still be too high for the weakest fault. Check minimum fault current, allowing for CT and relay tolerance.

9. Leaving too little time interval between relays. A commonly cited illustrative CTI is 0.2–0.4 s; the real value depends on breaker clearing time, relay overshoot and tolerances.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a plug (pickup) setting on an overcurrent relay? +

The minimum current, as a percentage of the relay's rated current (usually the CT secondary), at which the relay starts to operate. Set it above maximum load current (with margin) and below the minimum fault current to be detected.

How do I convert a pickup current into a plug setting percentage and relay amps? +

Plug Setting (%) = (Pickup Current ÷ CT Primary) × 100. Relay-side amps = plug setting % × relay rated current (1 A or 5 A). Example: 375 A on a 400/5 A CT = 93.75% = about 4.69 A. Then round to a setting the relay offers.

What is Plug Setting Multiplier (PSM) and why does it matter for relay settings? +

PSM = Fault Current ÷ Pickup Current. It sets the operating point on the curve used to calculate TMS. Use the actual selected pickup, not the theoretical one. For equations and trip times, use the TOC/IDMT Trip Time Calculator.

How do I choose the right pickup current margin above normal load? +

1.25-1.3 × maximum load is a common starting margin for a quick estimate, not a universal rule. The real margin depends on motor starting/inrush, transformer energization, permitted overload, relay/CT accuracy and coordination, so confirm it for the application.

Why does the calculator select the next higher plug tap instead of the nearest one? +

A lower tap shrinks the margin over maximum load and risks nuisance tripping. This calculator uses the next higher tap as a simplified method. A higher tap also lowers PSM and sensitivity, so check minimum fault current and the taps your relay actually offers.

What does Time Multiplier Setting (TMS) actually control? +

TMS scales the whole time-current curve: a higher TMS gives a longer operating time at every PSM, a lower TMS gives a faster one. It is the main tool for staggering relays in series.

How is the required TMS calculated for a desired operating time? +

For IEC Standard Inverse, TMS = Desired Operating Time ÷ [0.14 ÷ (PSM^0.02 − 1)]. At PSM 10 the time at TMS 1.0 is 2.971 s, so a 0.5 s target needs TMS ≈ 0.168. Use the value recalculated at the actual tap.

Which IDMT curve should I select when setting a relay? +

SI is the general-purpose choice; VI typically suits feeders where fault current falls sharply with distance; EI suits fuse coordination and inrush or cold-load pickup. Match the curve configured in the relay, because TMS is not transferable between curves. Constants and comparison are in the TOC/IDMT Trip Time Calculator.

Why is protection coordination between multiple relays more complex than setting one relay? +

A fault should trip only the device closest to it. That needs operating times compared for every device across a range of fault currents, which is far more involved than one relay's setting. This calculator covers a single relay only.

Can a numerical relay use the calculated pickup and TMS exactly? +

Often much closer. Many numerical relays allow fine or continuous pickup and TMS (the Continuous option here), but ranges and steps vary by model, so check your relay's limits and configured curve.

How can I check the actual trip time an already-configured relay setting will produce? +

This calculator works backward from a desired time to TMS and pickup. To enter an existing pickup, TMS/TD and fault current and get the trip time (any IEC/IEEE curve), use the TOC/IDMT Relay Trip Time Calculator.

How it works

How Voltage Drop Is Calculated for a Cable Run

Created by Umasankar Maity — B.Tech in Electrical Engineering, with 11+ years of industrial experience.

Reviewed by the ElectroMechCalc editorial team, comprising a qualified electrical engineer, a mechanical engineer, and a Chartered Accountant.

Last reviewed: August 2026  |  Standards referenced: Standard resistive voltage drop formula (IS 732 / IEC 60364-5-52 style, resistance-based)

Every conductor has some resistance, and any current flowing through that resistance produces a voltage drop — the voltage at the load end of a cable is always somewhat lower than at the source end. For short, heavily-loaded circuits this drop is negligible, but for long cable runs (a distant pump house, a remote lighting circuit, a large facility's far corner) it can become large enough to affect equipment performance, which is why voltage drop is checked as a standard part of cable sizing, alongside ampacity.

Formula used (single-phase): VD (V) = 2 × I × L × R ÷ 1000, where I is load current in amps, L is the one-way cable length in metres, and R is the conductor's resistance in ohms per kilometre. The factor of 2 accounts for the round trip — current flows out through one conductor and back through the other, so the total resistive path is twice the one-way length.

Formula used (three-phase): VD (V) = √3 × I × L × R ÷ 1000. The √3 (approximately 1.732) factor replaces the single-phase factor of 2, reflecting the different current-voltage relationship in a balanced three-phase system compared to a single-phase circuit.

Finding conductor resistance: R (Ω/km) = resistivity ÷ cross-sectional area. Using standard design values of approximately 17.5 Ω·mm²/m for copper and 28 Ω·mm²/m for aluminum (both already divided by 1000 to give ohms per km when divided by area in mm²), a 16 mm² copper conductor has R = 17.5 ÷ 16 ≈ 1.09 Ω/km.

Worked example: a single-phase circuit carries 40 A over a 60 m one-way cable run, using 16 mm² copper conductor (R ≈ 1.09 Ω/km), on a 230 V supply. VD = 2 × 40 × 60 × 1.09 ÷ 1000 ≈ 5.23 V. Percentage voltage drop = 5.23 ÷ 230 × 100 ≈ 2.27% — within the commonly used 5% total guideline, but worth checking against the specific limit for this particular circuit type (motor starting circuits, for example, often need a tighter limit).

Why voltage drop matters beyond just meeting a code limit: excessive voltage drop reduces the actual voltage available at the load, which has real performance consequences depending on the equipment. Motors draw more current to deliver the same output power at reduced voltage, running hotter and less efficiently. Lighting can dim and, for some lamp types, shift color temperature. Electronic equipment can behave erratically, trip on undervoltage protection, or in severe cases fail to start reliably. These aren't just theoretical code-compliance concerns — they're practical reliability and efficiency issues that voltage drop calculations exist to prevent.

The relationship between voltage drop and conductor upsizing: since voltage drop is inversely proportional to conductor area (larger area, proportionally lower resistance, proportionally lower voltage drop for the same current and length), the standard remedy for excessive voltage drop is simply increasing conductor size beyond what ampacity alone would require. This is a common and often economically significant outcome for long cable runs — a circuit feeding a remote pump house or a distant lighting panel frequently needs a conductor one or two sizes larger than its ampacity requirement alone would suggest, purely to keep voltage drop within acceptable limits.

Impedance vs pure resistance — when the difference matters: the formula used here treats the conductor's opposition to current flow as purely resistive, which is accurate for smaller conductors where the inductive reactance component is negligible. For larger conductors (typically above roughly 95-120 mm², though the exact threshold depends on the standard and installation method), reactance becomes a meaningful fraction of total impedance, and a more accurate voltage drop calculation uses the full formula VD = √3 × I × L × (R×cosφ + X×sinφ) ÷ 1000 for three-phase (with an analogous single-phase version), incorporating both resistance (R) and reactance (X) along with the load's power factor angle (φ). Cable manufacturers publish both R and X values per km for their larger cable sizes specifically because this full calculation becomes necessary at that scale.

Summary: use VD = 2 × I × L × R ÷ 1000 for single-phase or VD = √3 × I × L × R ÷ 1000 for three-phase, calculate R from your conductor's cross-sectional area and material (or use the manufacturer's published value directly for the most accuracy), check the result against the appropriate percentage limit for your specific circuit type, and always check voltage drop alongside ampacity, using whichever constraint requires the larger conductor.

Voltage drop in DC systems and battery-based installations: the same underlying resistive voltage drop principle applies to DC circuits (solar PV wiring, battery banks, DC-powered equipment), using VD = 2 × I × L × R ÷ 1000 — the same formula as single-phase AC, since DC also requires a complete circuit with current flowing out and back. DC systems are often particularly voltage-drop sensitive because they typically operate at lower voltages (12V, 24V, 48V systems are common), meaning the same absolute voltage drop represents a much larger percentage of the total system voltage than it would in a 230V or 415V AC system — DC cable runs often need to be kept shorter or use proportionally larger conductors for exactly this reason.

Voltage drop and energy losses: voltage drop isn't just a performance concern for the load — the power dissipated in the cable as I²R losses (heat) represents real, wasted energy that's paid for but never reaches the load. For circuits that carry substantial current over long distances for extended periods (a continuously running large motor on a long feeder, for example), the cumulative cost of this resistive loss over the equipment's service life can sometimes justify upsizing the conductor beyond the minimum required by either ampacity or the standard voltage drop limit, purely on energy-cost grounds — a more detailed lifecycle cost calculation, weighing the extra upfront conductor cost against ongoing energy savings, can identify this optimal point for high-utilization circuits.

Worked Example

Single-phase, 40 A, 60 m, 16 mm² copper (R≈1.09 Ω/km), 230 V: VD = 2 × 40 × 60 × 1.09 ÷ 1000 ≈ 5.23 V (≈ 2.27%).

This calculator uses a resistance-based voltage drop formula, which is accurate for smaller conductor sizes where reactance is negligible relative to resistance. For larger conductors (commonly above 95-120 mm² depending on the standard) or long cable runs at low power factor, reactance becomes significant and a full impedance-based calculation (using both R and X, with the load's power factor angle) gives a more accurate result — consult your cable manufacturer's published R and X values per km for a precise calculation on larger installations. For a final design, particularly one involving long runs, large conductors, or voltage-sensitive equipment, have the calculation reviewed by a qualified electrical engineer against the specific code requirements in force for your installation.

Design Limit

Recommended Maximum Voltage Drop by Application

Application Recommended Max Voltage Drop
Feeder / distribution circuit3%
Final branch circuit2%
Total (source to furthest load)5%
Motor starting circuits10-15% momentary (starting only)
Sensitive electronic/IT equipment1-2% (tighter, check equipment spec)

The commonly cited 5% total figure is a general guideline, not a universal legal limit in every jurisdiction — some codes specify it as a recommendation rather than a hard requirement, while others make it mandatory for certain circuit types. Motor starting is a special case: momentary voltage drop during the brief high-inrush starting period is generally allowed to be much larger than the steady-state running limit, since it's transient and doesn't represent a sustained condition — but excessive starting voltage drop can still prevent a motor from developing enough torque to actually start and accelerate to full speed.

When designing a motor circuit specifically, check starting voltage drop separately from running voltage drop using the motor's starting (locked-rotor) current, which is typically 5-8× its full-load running current — a cable sized only against running current voltage drop can still produce unacceptable starting voltage drop, since starting current is so much higher even though it only flows briefly.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Calculating Voltage Drop

1. Using total cable length (both directions) instead of one-way length. The formula's factor of 2 (single-phase) or √3 (three-phase) already accounts for the return path — entering the round-trip length instead of one-way length double-counts this and overstates voltage drop.

2. Using the wrong resistivity constant for the actual conductor material. Copper and aluminum have meaningfully different resistivity (aluminum's resistance is roughly 1.6× copper's for the same cross-sectional area) — using copper's constant for an aluminum conductor, or vice versa, gives a significantly wrong result.

3. Ignoring reactance for large conductors. This resistance-only calculation is accurate for smaller conductors, but for larger sizes (commonly above 95-120 mm²) reactance becomes a meaningful fraction of total impedance — using resistance alone for large-conductor, long-run calculations can understate actual voltage drop.

4. Checking voltage drop at a light or average load instead of full rated load. Voltage drop scales directly with current — a calculation done at typical operating current rather than the circuit's full designed load current can pass a check that would actually fail under legitimate full-load conditions.

5. Sizing a cable for ampacity alone without also checking voltage drop. A conductor that's thermally adequate (correct ampacity) for a long run can still produce excessive voltage drop — always check both constraints and use whichever requires the larger conductor size, especially for long cable runs with relatively light current.

6. Confusing percentage voltage drop limits meant for steady-state operation with motor starting conditions. Motor starting circuits are commonly allowed a much larger momentary voltage drop than steady-state limits suggest — applying a tight steady-state limit to a starting-current calculation can lead to an unnecessarily oversized (and expensive) cable for the starting condition alone.

7. Ignoring starting current voltage drop for motor circuits. Motor starting (locked-rotor) current is typically 5-8× full-load running current — checking voltage drop only at running current misses a potentially much larger, if brief, starting voltage drop that can prevent the motor from starting reliably.

8. Not accounting for the same conductor's temperature-dependent resistance change. Conductor resistance increases with temperature — the standard resistivity values used in most quick calculations are typically referenced at a specific temperature (often 20°C or the conductor's rated operating temperature); a cable running hot under load has somewhat higher actual resistance, and therefore somewhat higher actual voltage drop, than a room-temperature calculation alone suggests.

A practical checklist for a complete cable sizing exercise: (1) determine the load's full rated current; (2) select a conductor size meeting ampacity requirements after applying temperature and grouping derating; (3) calculate voltage drop at that conductor size and full load current; (4) if voltage drop exceeds the applicable limit, increase conductor size and recheck both ampacity margin and voltage drop; (5) for motor circuits, additionally check starting current voltage drop separately. Working through these steps in order, rather than checking only one constraint, is what a complete and defensible cable sizing calculation actually requires.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for voltage drop in a cable run? +

For single-phase: VD (V) = 2 × I × L × R ÷ 1000. For three-phase: VD (V) = √3 × I × L × R ÷ 1000. Where I is load current (A), L is one-way cable length (m), and R is conductor resistance in ohms per km. The factor of 2 for single-phase accounts for both the outgoing and return conductor; three-phase uses √3 instead, reflecting the phase relationship between the three conductors.

How do I find the resistance value (R) for my conductor? +

R (ohm/km) = resistivity ÷ cross-sectional area, using approximately 17.5 for copper and 28 for aluminum (both in Ω·mm²/m, divided by area in mm²) as commonly used design values — or look up the exact published resistance for your specific cable from its manufacturer's datasheet, which is more accurate since it accounts for stranding and actual material composition.

What is an acceptable percentage voltage drop? +

A widely used general guideline limits total voltage drop from source to the furthest load to 5% (often split as roughly 3% for the feeder/distribution run and 2% for the final branch circuit), though some codes and applications specify tighter limits, particularly for sensitive electronic equipment or motor starting circuits.

Why does three-phase use √3 instead of 2 in the voltage drop formula? +

The factor of 2 in single-phase voltage drop accounts for current flowing out on one conductor and back on another (effectively doubling the resistive path). In a balanced three-phase system, the vector relationship between the three phase currents and voltages means the equivalent multiplying factor works out to √3 (approximately 1.732) instead of 2, which is a standard result from three-phase circuit analysis.

Does voltage drop depend on power factor? +

For the resistance-only calculation used here, no — power factor doesn't directly enter the formula. However, for larger conductors where reactance (X) is significant relative to resistance, the full voltage drop formula includes both R×cosφ and X×sinφ terms, meaning power factor does affect voltage drop meaningfully once reactance can no longer be ignored.

Why does using a larger conductor reduce voltage drop? +

Resistance is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area (R = resistivity ÷ area) — a larger conductor has proportionally lower resistance, and since voltage drop is directly proportional to resistance, doubling conductor area roughly halves voltage drop for the same current and length. This is why voltage drop, not just ampacity, often determines the minimum acceptable conductor size for a long cable run.

Can voltage drop exceed thermal ampacity as the limiting factor in cable sizing? +

Yes, and this is common for long cable runs with relatively light loads — a cable sized purely for ampacity might carry the required current safely from a heating perspective, but still produce excessive voltage drop over a long run, requiring a larger conductor than ampacity alone would suggest. Always check both ampacity and voltage drop, and use whichever requires the larger conductor size.

Does voltage drop affect motor performance? +

Yes, significantly — motor torque is proportional to the square of applied voltage, so even a modest voltage drop can cause a disproportionately larger reduction in available starting and running torque, along with increased current draw (and heating) as the motor works harder to deliver the same mechanical output at reduced voltage. Motor starting circuits, where inrush current is highest, are often the most voltage-drop-sensitive part of a design.

Should I calculate voltage drop at full load current or actual operating current? +

For circuit design and code compliance, calculate at the full rated load current the circuit is designed to carry, since this represents the worst-case condition the installation must handle safely — checking only at typical or average operating current could pass a calculation that fails under legitimate peak/full-load conditions.

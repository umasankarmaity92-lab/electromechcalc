How it works

How Star-Delta Starter Contactors Are Sized

Created by Umasankar Maity — B.Tech in Electrical Engineering, with 11+ years of industrial experience.

Reviewed by the ElectroMechCalc editorial team, comprising a qualified electrical engineer, a mechanical engineer, and a Chartered Accountant.

Last reviewed: August 2026  |  Standards referenced: Standard star-delta starter contactor sizing convention

A star-delta starter uses three contactors working together in sequence — the main contactor, the star contactor, and the delta contactor — to first connect the motor windings in a reduced-voltage star configuration for starting, then switch to the normal full-voltage delta configuration for running. Each contactor experiences different current duty, and correctly sizing each one individually (rather than using one uniform rating for all three) is standard, economical starter design practice.

Formula used: Main Contactor Rating = FLC × 1.0. Delta Contactor Rating = FLC × 0.58. Star Contactor Rating = FLC × 0.33. These multipliers reflect each contactor's actual current duty within the star-delta switching arrangement, not simply a uniform safety margin applied identically to all three.

Worked example: a motor with 60 A full load current. Main Contactor = 60 × 1.0 = 60 A. Delta Contactor = 60 × 0.58 = 34.8 A. Star Contactor = 60 × 0.33 = 19.8 A. Notice the delta and star contactors can both be meaningfully smaller (and less expensive) than the main contactor, since neither carries the full line current the main contactor handles.

Why the delta contactor carries only 0.58 (roughly 1÷√3) of line current: the delta contactor sits in series with two of the motor's three windings within the delta connection, carrying winding (phase) current rather than the full line current — in a delta-connected three-phase load, phase current relates to line current by a factor of 1÷√3 ≈ 0.577, which rounds to the commonly used 0.58 design figure.

Why the star contactor carries only about 0.33 of line current: the star contactor carries the star-connection starting current, which (as established in star-delta starting theory) is one-third of the equivalent delta/DOL line current — since this contactor only operates briefly during starting and at this reduced current level, it can be sized considerably smaller than either the main or delta contactor, contributing to the overall economy of a properly-specified star-delta starter panel.

The complete star-delta switching sequence: at start, the main contactor and star contactor close together, connecting the motor windings in star configuration and applying supply voltage — the motor begins accelerating at reduced torque and current. After the timer's set duration elapses, the star contactor opens (briefly disconnecting the star point) and the delta contactor closes, reconnecting the windings in delta configuration at full line voltage, with the main contactor remaining closed throughout to maintain the supply connection. This sequencing, along with a brief interlock delay between opening the star contactor and closing the delta contactor (to prevent the two from ever being closed simultaneously, which would create a dangerous short circuit across the windings), is typically handled automatically by a dedicated star-delta timer relay or a programmable logic controller in more sophisticated installations.

Why the star and delta contactors must never be closed simultaneously: if both the star and delta contactors were closed at the same time, the motor windings would be simultaneously connected in two incompatible configurations, creating a direct short circuit path through the windings and potentially causing severe damage to the motor and starter equipment. This is precisely why star-delta timer relays include a mandatory brief interlock delay (commonly 50-100 milliseconds) between opening the star contactor and closing the delta contactor, ensuring a clean break before the new connection is made, along with electrical and sometimes mechanical interlocking between the two contactors as an additional safety measure against this failure mode.

Duty cycle considerations for the star contactor specifically: because the star contactor only operates during the brief starting period and is de-energized during normal running, it experiences meaningfully less operating time and switching duty than the main or delta contactor over the starter's service life — this is part of the justification for its smaller, more economical sizing, since its actual electrical endurance requirement (total switching operations over its service life) is inherently lower than a contactor that remains energized continuously during normal motor operation.

Summary: use Main = FLC × 1.0, Delta = FLC × 0.58, Star = FLC × 0.33 for the three contactors, set the star timer based on actual observed motor acceleration during commissioning (typically 3-10 seconds as a starting reference range), ensure proper interlocking prevents star and delta contactors from ever closing simultaneously, and remember the three contactors handle switching only — separate overload and short-circuit protection is still required.

Comparing star-delta to newer alternatives: soft starters and VFDs have increasingly displaced star-delta starting in many new installations, offering smoother, more adjustable starting current and torque control without the mechanical complexity of three separate contactors and a precisely-timed switching sequence. Star-delta starters remain common, however, particularly for retrofit situations, simpler and more cost-sensitive applications, or facilities standardized on this well-understood, mechanically robust technology — the choice between star-delta and its electronic alternatives typically comes down to a trade-off between star-delta's lower upfront cost and mechanical simplicity versus soft starters' and VFDs' smoother performance and additional capability (like speed control, for VFDs specifically).

Panel layout and wiring considerations: a star-delta starter panel needs six motor leads brought out to the starter (rather than the three leads a simple DOL starter needs), since all six winding ends must be individually accessible to implement both the star and delta connections — this means star-delta starter wiring is inherently more involved than a simple DOL arrangement, both in terms of the motor cable itself (six conductors instead of three) and the internal starter panel wiring connecting the three contactors to the appropriate winding terminals in the correct sequence.

Worked Example

FLC=60A: Main Contactor = 60×1.0 = 60 A. Delta Contactor = 60×0.58 = 34.8 A. Star Contactor = 60×0.33 = 19.8 A.

Contactor sizing multipliers here (1.0× for main, 0.58× for delta, 0.33× for star) are the standard, widely used star-delta starter design convention — always cross-check final contactor selection against the manufacturer's AC-3 rated current table for your exact contactor model, and confirm timer settings against your specific motor's actual acceleration characteristics, ideally verified during commissioning. Ensure reliable interlocking between star and delta contactors, and confirm the motor is genuinely wound and terminated for star-delta operation before specifying this starter type.

Setup Guidance

Setting the Star Timer for Reliable Star-to-Delta Transition

The star timer is what controls exactly when the starter transitions from star (reduced-voltage starting) to delta (full-voltage running) configuration — it should be set long enough for the motor to accelerate close to its normal running speed while still in the lower-torque star configuration, but not needlessly longer than that. As a general guideline, smaller motors with light-inertia loads often need only 3-5 seconds, while larger motors or higher-inertia loads (large fans, big pumps, heavy conveyors) may need 6-10 seconds or occasionally more to reach adequate speed before transitioning.

The correct setting for any specific installation ultimately depends on the actual motor and load combination's real acceleration behavior, not a generic assumed value — commissioning technicians typically start with a reasonable estimate based on motor size and load type, then observe actual motor sound and current behavior during the transition (a smooth, modest transition current bump indicates good timing; a large, harsh current spike suggests the timer needs adjustment).

Documenting the final commissioned timer setting, along with the specific motor and load combination it was verified for, is worthwhile for future reference — if the motor or driven load is ever changed, the previously verified timer setting shouldn't be assumed to still be correct without re-verification, since acceleration behavior depends on the specific combination of motor characteristics and load inertia/torque requirement, not just the starter panel's fixed hardware configuration.

Field troubleshooting a star-delta starter: common real-world issues include a failed or misadjusted timer (motor stuck in star indefinitely, running permanently underpowered, or transitioning far too early), a worn or damaged star or delta contactor (given its cyclic switching duty, especially the star contactor which sees repeated starting-only operation), or a wiring error in the six-lead motor connection (which can cause the motor to run backward, overheat, or fail to start correctly if winding connections are swapped or incorrect). Systematically checking timer operation, contactor condition, and connection correctness in that general order is a practical troubleshooting sequence for a star-delta starter that's stopped working correctly or is exhibiting unusual starting behavior.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Sizing a Star-Delta Starter

1. Sizing all three contactors identically to the main contactor rating. This wastes money on unnecessarily oversized star and delta contactors — each contactor has a distinctly different current duty and should be sized according to its own specific multiplier, not uniformly to the highest (main contactor) figure.

2. Confusing the 0.58 delta contactor factor with the 0.33 star contactor factor, or vice versa. These are genuinely different figures for genuinely different contactors — mixing them up undersizes one contactor while unnecessarily oversizing the other.

3. Setting the star timer without observing actual motor acceleration behavior. A generic assumed timer setting may not suit your specific motor and load combination — always verify and adjust the actual setting during commissioning based on observed motor behavior, not just a rule-of-thumb starting point alone.

4. Assuming star-delta starting is suitable for any driven load without checking starting torque requirements. Star-delta starting provides only about a third of DOL starting torque — loads with high starting torque requirements (some conveyors, positive-displacement pumps, high-inertia fans) may not accelerate properly under star-delta starting regardless of correct contactor sizing or timer setting.

5. Omitting overload and short-circuit protection because "the contactors provide protection." The three contactors handle switching only, not overcurrent protection — a complete star-delta starter still needs a properly sized overload relay and upstream fuse or MCCB, exactly as any other motor starter arrangement does.

6. Not confirming the motor is actually wound and terminal-configured for star-delta operation. This requires a motor with all six winding ends brought out to allow both star and delta connection at its rated running voltage — not every motor is built this way, and attempting a star-delta starter on an incompatible motor isn't possible without rewinding or replacing it.

7. Missing or inadequate interlocking between star and delta contactors. Without reliable electrical and/or mechanical interlocking preventing simultaneous closure, a control fault or timer malfunction could close both contactors together, creating a dangerous short circuit through the motor windings — proper interlocking is a safety-critical, not optional, part of star-delta starter design.

8. Reusing a previously verified timer setting after changing the motor or driven load. Acceleration behavior depends on the specific motor and load combination — a timer setting verified correct for one configuration shouldn't be assumed valid after a motor replacement or significant load change without re-verification.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does a star-delta starter need three separate contactors? +

The main contactor connects the supply to the motor's winding terminals and carries full running current continuously. The star contactor temporarily connects the winding ends together in a star configuration only during starting. The delta contactor connects the windings in their normal running (delta) configuration once starting is complete — together, these three contactors implement the switch from reduced-voltage star starting to full-voltage delta running.

What is the standard sizing rule for the main contactor? +

The main contactor should be rated at or above the motor's full load line current (FLC), since it carries the complete motor running current continuously once the motor transitions to normal delta operation and throughout normal running.

What is the standard sizing rule for the delta contactor? +

The delta contactor should be rated at approximately 0.58 (58%) of the motor's full load line current, since it sits in series with two of the three motor windings in the delta configuration, carrying phase current rather than the full line current the main contactor sees.

What is the standard sizing rule for the star contactor? +

The star contactor should be rated at approximately 0.33 (33%) of the motor's full load line current, since it only carries the reduced star-connection starting current, and only briefly during the starting period — its duty is lighter and shorter than either the main or delta contactor's duty.

How long should the star timer be set for? +

The star timer should be set long enough for the motor to reach close to its full running speed while still in star connection, but not excessively longer than necessary — typical settings range from about 3 to 10 seconds depending on motor size and the driven load's inertia, though the correct setting for any specific installation should be verified by observing actual motor acceleration behavior during commissioning, not just assumed from a generic range.

What happens if the star timer is set too short? +

Transitioning to delta before the motor has reached adequate speed causes a second, more severe current and torque transient at the transition moment, since the motor is still accelerating rather than running near full speed — this can partially defeat the purpose of star-delta starting's reduced-current benefit and cause additional mechanical stress.

What happens if the star timer is set too long? +

The motor continues running in the reduced-torque, reduced-current star configuration longer than necessary, which is generally less harmful than switching too early, but for loads with substantial starting torque requirements, an excessively long star period can mean the motor never actually reaches full speed in star configuration, potentially stalling or drawing sustained high current without ever accelerating properly.

Can a star-delta starter be used with a soft starter or VFD for a smoother transition? +

Star-delta starters are a distinct, older technology from soft starters and VFDs — a soft starter or VFD generally replaces a star-delta arrangement entirely rather than being combined with it, since both achieve reduced-current starting through fundamentally different means (electronic voltage/frequency control rather than mechanical winding reconfiguration) that aren't typically used together on the same motor.

Does a star-delta starter need any additional protective devices beyond the three contactors? +

Yes — a star-delta starter still needs overcurrent/overload protection (typically an overload relay, sized to the motor's full load running current) and short-circuit protection (a fuse or MCCB), plus the timer relay controlling the star-to-delta transition sequence — the three contactors alone only handle the switching function, not protection.

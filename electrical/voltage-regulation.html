How it works

How to Calculate Transformer Voltage Regulation

A 500 kVA, 415 V transformer with %R = 1.2% and %X = 4.8% supplying full load at 0.8 power factor lagging has a voltage regulation of about 4.05% when referred to the full-load voltage (3.89% when referred to the rated no-load voltage) — its secondary voltage falls from 415 V to about 398.9 V. Voltage regulation is simply this change in secondary terminal voltage between no load and a given load, with the primary voltage held constant, expressed as a percentage.

The change happens because load current flows through the transformer's own winding resistance and leakage reactance. How much of that internal drop actually shows up as a change in terminal voltage depends strongly on the load's power factor, which is why the same transformer can show a few percent of voltage drop with a motor load and a small voltage rise with a capacitive load.

The terminal voltage VFL plus the resistive drop (in phase with the current) and the reactive drop (90° ahead of the current) add up to the open-circuit voltage VNL. The horizontal projection of the two drops is the first-order approximation; the small gap between that projection and the VNL arc is what the second-order and exact methods add.

Definition: Two Reference Conventions

Voltage regulation is always based on the difference VNL − VFL, but two reference voltages are in common use. Many textbooks divide by the full-load voltage: VR = (VNL − VFL) ÷ VFL × 100. A rated-voltage basis is common in IEC-oriented transformer calculations and in some utility references, expressing the same drop as a percentage of the rated (no-load) voltage: VR = (VNL − VFL) ÷ VNL × 100. The second gives a slightly smaller number. Neither is wrong — just state which one you are using. The calculator lets you choose, and the breakdown always shows both.

Method 1: First-Order Approximation

For quick work the drop is usually taken as the horizontal projection of the impedance-drop phasors onto the terminal voltage:

VR ≈ %R × cosφ + %X × sinφ (lagging)   or   VR ≈ %R × cosφ − %X × sinφ (leading)

Multiply by the load fraction (for example 0.75 at 75% load) for part-load conditions. This form ignores the small vertical (quadrature) component of the drop, so it slightly under-reads at low power factor or high impedance. For the example transformer it gives 1.2 × 0.8 + 4.8 × 0.6 = 3.84%.

Method 2: IEC Second-Order Formula

The application guide IEC 60076-8 adds a second-order term that accounts for the quadrature component. With a = load fraction, er′ = a(%R cosφ + %X sinφ) and ex′ = a(%X cosφ − %R sinφ) for lagging load (swap the signs of the sinφ terms for leading load):

VR (% of rated voltage) = er′ + (ex′)² ÷ 200

For the example, ex′ = 4.8 × 0.8 − 1.2 × 0.6 = 3.12, so VR = 3.84 + 3.12² ÷ 200 = 3.889% of the rated voltage — practically identical to the exact solution on the same basis.

Method 3: Exact Phasor Solution

Holding the no-load (open-circuit) voltage fixed at 1 per unit, the full-load terminal voltage is found by solving the phasor triangle exactly. With the per-unit terms A = a(r cosφ ± x sinφ) and B = a(x cosφ ∓ r sinφ), where r = %R ÷ 100 and x = %X ÷ 100 (upper signs lagging, lower signs leading):

VFL (pu) = −A + √(1 − B²)

For the example: A = 0.0384, B = 0.0312, so VFL = −0.0384 + √(1 − 0.0312²) = 0.96111 pu = 398.9 V. Then VR = (1 − 0.96111) ÷ 0.96111 = 4.05% of VFL, or 3.89% of VNL. This is the value the calculator shows as its main result.

Converting Ohms to %R and %X Correctly

Test reports sometimes give the equivalent resistance and reactance in ohms. To convert, divide by the base impedance on the same side: Zbase = VLL² ÷ (kVA × 1000) for a three-phase transformer (per-phase, star-equivalent ohms) or V² ÷ (kVA × 1000) for single-phase. Then %R = Req ÷ Zbase × 100 and %X = Xeq ÷ Zbase × 100. Equivalently, %R = IFL × Req ÷ Vphase × 100 — note it is the phase voltage (VLL ÷ √3), not the line voltage, for a three-phase transformer. For the 500 kVA, 415 V example, Zbase = 415² ÷ 500,000 = 0.3445 Ω, so 1.2% and 4.8% correspond to Req = 0.00413 Ω and Xeq = 0.01653 Ω per phase.

If only the nameplate %Z (impedance voltage) is known, %R can be found from the measured load loss, %R = load loss (W) ÷ rated VA × 100, and then %X = √(%Z² − %R²).

Worked Example

500 kVA, 415 V, %R = 1.2, %X = 4.8, full load at 0.8 pf lagging → approximate VR = 3.84%; IEC second-order = 3.89% of VNL; exact VFL = 0.96111 × 415 = 398.9 V, VR = 4.05% of VFL. Same load at 0.8 pf leading → VFL = 422.5 V, VR = −1.78% (voltage rises).

A regulation figure only means something against what the load can tolerate, so the calculator adds a plain-language band to every result. The bands come from how real distribution and power transformers usually behave on load — they are a practical guide, not limits taken from a standard.

What finally matters is the voltage at the equipment, which is the supply voltage minus the transformer regulation and the cable drop together. Supply-voltage tolerances are set by the applicable standard and your utility — for example, IEC 60038 gives ±10% for 230/400 V systems at the supply terminals, and ANSI C84.1 Range A allows ±5% on 120 V-base service voltage — so a “High” transformer regulation may still be acceptable with short cables, while a “Typical” one can be a problem at the end of a long feeder.

Worst-Case and Zero-Regulation Power Factor

Regulation is highest at the lagging power factor where the load current is in phase with the transformer's impedance, tanφ = %X ÷ %R, i.e. cosφ = %R ÷ %Z. There the whole impedance drop lines up with the terminal voltage, so in the exact solution the regulation at 100% load equals %Z on the rated-voltage basis; at a load fraction a it equals a × %Z. For the example at full load that is cosφ ≈ 0.24 lagging, with 4.95% of the rated voltage, or 5.21% of the on-load voltage. Regulation passes through zero at approximately cosφ = %X ÷ %Z leading (the first-order condition tanφ = %R ÷ %X) — about 0.97 leading for the example; the exact crossing is slightly more leading, at about 0.964. Beyond that point the secondary voltage rises as load increases, the same mechanism that causes voltage rise on an over-compensated power factor correction bank or on a lightly loaded long cable or line (the Ferranti effect).

Part Load and Overload

The impedance drop is proportional to current, so over normal operating ranges the first-order part of the regulation scales approximately linearly with load — at 50% load it is roughly half the full-load figure. The exact solution also includes the small quadrature effect, which is not linear. The calculator applies the load percentage inside the exact solution, so overload conditions (up to 200%) are handled too.

Why Transformers Are Not Built with Very Low %Z

A lower %X would improve regulation, but it would also raise the prospective short-circuit current, since fault current is roughly rated current ÷ (%Z ÷ 100). Transformer impedance is therefore a compromise between voltage regulation and fault level. IEC 60076-5 (Table 1 of the 2006 edition) gives recognised minimum short-circuit impedance values:

Keeping Supply Voltage Within Limits

When regulation pushes the load voltage too low, the usual remedies are: adjust the off-circuit tap (with the transformer de-energised) or use an on-load tap changer; improve the load power factor with capacitors, keeping clear of over-compensation at light load; and check that the cable voltage drop from the transformer to the load is not the larger part of the problem. See the cable voltage drop calculator, the guide to common transformer sizing mistakes, and the transformer maintenance guide.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Calculating Voltage Regulation

1. Using line voltage with per-phase ohms. For a three-phase transformer, %R = IFL × Req ÷ Vphase. Dividing by the line-to-line voltage instead understates %R and %X by a factor of √3 and gives a regulation that is far too low.

2. Mixing ohms referred to different sides. Primary and secondary resistances must both be referred to the same side (multiply or divide by the turns ratio squared) before they are added. Adding a primary ohm value directly to a secondary one gives a meaningless result.

3. Using the lagging sign for a leading load. The sign of the %X sinφ term flips with the type of load. A capacitive load treated as lagging hides a real voltage rise.

4. Not stating the reference voltage. A result divided by VFL and one divided by VNL differ slightly. Quoting 4.05% against a specification written on the rated-voltage basis (3.89%) causes needless disputes.

5. Treating %Z as the regulation. %Z is the impedance voltage at rated current. Regulation only approaches %Z at a very low lagging power factor; at 0.8–1.0 power factor it is well below %Z.

6. Ignoring load percentage. Full-load regulation quoted for a transformer running at 40% load overstates the actual voltage change by more than double.

7. Blaming the transformer for cable voltage drop. Voltage measured at a distant distribution board includes the cable drop. Measure at the transformer terminals when checking regulation.

8. Comparing measurements taken on different taps or supply voltages. A no-load reading taken on one day and a full-load reading on another, with a different incoming voltage or tap setting, measures the network as well as the transformer.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for transformer voltage regulation? +

Voltage regulation is the change in secondary terminal voltage from no load to a given load, with the primary voltage held constant: VR = (V_NL − V_FL) ÷ V_FL × 100 when referred to the full-load voltage, or (V_NL − V_FL) ÷ V_NL × 100 when referred to the rated (no-load) voltage. From the transformer's impedance it is commonly approximated as VR ≈ %R × cosφ + %X × sinφ for a lagging load, or %R × cosφ − %X × sinφ for a leading load.

How do I calculate voltage regulation from %R and %X? +

Take %R and %X from the test report (or work out %X = √(%Z² − %R²) from the nameplate %Z), find sinφ = √(1 − pf²), and apply %R × cosφ ± %X × sinφ, multiplied by the load fraction for part load. For higher accuracy add the second-order term (%X cosφ ∓ %R sinφ)² ÷ 200, or solve the phasor triangle exactly as this calculator does. For %R = 1.2, %X = 4.8 at 0.8 pf lagging and full load, the approximation gives 3.84% and the exact solution 3.89% of the rated voltage.

What is the difference between the approximate, IEC and exact methods? +

The approximate method uses only the horizontal projection of the impedance drops, so it slightly under-reads at low power factor or high impedance. The IEC 60076-8 second-order formula adds a term for the quadrature component and is almost exact for normal transformers. The exact method solves the equivalent-circuit phasor triangle directly. For typical distribution transformers at 0.8–1.0 power factor all three agree to within a few hundredths of a percent.

Should voltage regulation be divided by the full-load or the no-load voltage? +

Both conventions are used. Many textbooks divide the voltage change by the full-load voltage, while a rated-voltage basis is common in IEC-oriented transformer calculations and in some utility references; it gives a slightly smaller figure. Neither is wrong; state which basis you are using, especially when comparing with a specification.

Can voltage regulation be negative? +

Yes. With a leading (capacitive) load the reactance term changes sign, and once the power factor is more leading than roughly cosφ = %X ÷ %Z the secondary voltage under load is higher than at no load, so regulation is negative. This is the same effect that causes voltage rise with over-compensated power factor correction capacitors at light load.

At what power factor is voltage regulation the highest? +

Regulation is greatest at the lagging power factor where tanφ = %X ÷ %R, which is cosφ = %R ÷ %Z. At that point the load current is in phase with the transformer's impedance, so at 100% load the exact regulation equals %Z on the rated-voltage basis (slightly more on the on-load-voltage basis); at a load fraction a it equals a × %Z. Because %X is usually much larger than %R, this worst case occurs at a very low lagging power factor.

How do I convert Req and Xeq in ohms to %R and %X? +

Divide each by the base impedance on the same side, Z_base = V² ÷ (kVA × 1000), using the line-to-line voltage and per-phase star-equivalent ohms for a three-phase transformer, and multiply by 100. Equivalently, %R = I_FL × R_eq ÷ V_phase × 100 — note that the phase voltage, not the line voltage, is used for a three-phase transformer.

Where do %R and %X values come from? +

Both come from the short-circuit (load-loss) test. The impedance voltage needed to circulate rated current gives %Z, the measured load loss gives %R = load loss ÷ rated VA × 100, and %X = √(%Z² − %R²). The values are listed on the transformer's test report, and %Z is usually on the nameplate.

What is a typical voltage regulation for a distribution transformer? +

It depends mainly on %Z and the load power factor. A distribution transformer with an impedance of about 4–6% typically shows a full-load regulation of roughly 3–5% at 0.8 power factor lagging, and around 1–2% at unity power factor. This calculator labels results as low (0–2%), typical (2–5%), high (5–8%) or very high (above 8%), with negative values shown as a voltage rise — a practical guide from typical transformer behaviour, not a standard limit. Check the actual figure with the transformer's own %R and %X.

Does voltage regulation change with load? +

Yes. The internal voltage drop is proportional to load current, so over normal operating ranges regulation scales approximately linearly with load — about half the full-load value at 50% load. The exact solution adds a small non-linear quadrature effect. This calculator applies the load percentage inside the exact solution, including overloads up to 200%.

How is transformer voltage regulation different from cable voltage drop? +

Voltage regulation is the change at the transformer's own secondary terminals caused by its internal impedance. Cable voltage drop is the further fall along the cable from the transformer to the load. The voltage a load actually receives depends on both, so a low reading at a distant board does not necessarily mean the transformer's regulation is poor — use the cable voltage drop calculator to separate the two.

Why don't manufacturers make transformer impedance very low to improve regulation? +

Lower %Z would reduce regulation, but it would also raise the prospective short-circuit current, since fault current is roughly rated current ÷ (%Z ÷ 100). That increases the breaking capacity and short-circuit withstand needed by switchgear and cables, and the stresses on the transformer itself. Impedance is therefore a balance between regulation and fault level, and IEC 60076-5 lists recognised minimum values by rating.

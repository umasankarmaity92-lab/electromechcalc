How it works

How Illuminance (Lux) Is Calculated from Lumens and Area

Created by Umasankar Maity — B.Tech in Electrical Engineering, with 11+ years of industrial experience.

Reviewed by the ElectroMechCalc editorial team, comprising a qualified electrical engineer, a mechanical engineer, and a Chartered Accountant.

Last reviewed: August 2026  |  Standards referenced: Standard illuminance formula (lux = lumens ÷ area)

Lux is the standard unit of illuminance — how much light actually falls on a surface, as opposed to lumens, which measure total light output from a source regardless of how spread out it is. The relationship between them is straightforward: spread a given amount of light (lumens) over a smaller area and illuminance (lux) goes up; spread the same lumens over a larger area and lux goes down.

Formula used: Illuminance (lux) = Total Luminous Flux (lumens) ÷ Illuminated Area (m²). This is literally the definition of lux — one lux equals one lumen spread evenly over one square metre.

Worked example: a room's lighting fixtures together produce 12,000 lumens of total luminous flux, illuminating a 30 m² area. Illuminance = 12,000 ÷ 30 = 400 lux — a level well-suited to general office and reading tasks, per standard lighting guidelines.

Why this is an average, not a point measurement: this calculation gives the average illuminance across the entire defined area, assuming lumens are distributed evenly — in reality, illuminance varies across a room, typically higher directly beneath or near light fixtures and lower in corners or areas further from any fixture. Actual lighting design accounts for this variation through proper fixture placement and spacing, and can be verified with a lux meter at specific points, but this average calculation remains a useful first-pass planning tool for estimating whether a given lumen output is roughly appropriate for a space of a given size.

Converting to foot-candles for regions using imperial units: 1 foot-candle (fc) ≈ 10.76 lux, so a lux value can be converted to foot-candles by dividing by roughly 10.76 — some regions and older lighting standards, particularly in North America, still reference foot-candles rather than lux, so this conversion is useful when comparing figures from different sources or standards.

Illuminance versus luminance — a commonly confused distinction: illuminance (lux) measures light falling onto a surface from a light source, while luminance (measured in candela per square metre) measures light actually reflected or emitted from a surface toward an observer's eye — these are related but distinct quantities, since a surface's reflectance (how much incident light it reflects versus absorbs) determines the relationship between the two. A brightly lit but very dark-colored (low-reflectance) surface can have high illuminance but relatively low luminance (appearing dim to an observer), which is why lighting design sometimes needs to consider both quantities, not just illuminance alone, for a complete understanding of how a space will actually appear and function visually.

Inverse square law and point-source illuminance: for a single point light source (rather than the area-average calculation this tool performs), illuminance actually follows an inverse square relationship with distance from the source — doubling the distance from a point source reduces illuminance to one-quarter of its value at the original distance, not simply half. This point-source relationship (E = I ÷ d², where I is luminous intensity in candela and d is distance) is the basis for more detailed photometric calculations involving individual fixture placement and spacing, distinct from this calculator's simpler area-average approach, which is well suited to a first-pass estimate for a space illuminated by multiple distributed fixtures.

Daylighting contribution to actual illuminance: for spaces with significant natural light (windows, skylights), actual daytime illuminance can be substantially higher than what artificial lighting alone would produce, sometimes to the point where artificial lighting can be dimmed or switched off during bright daylight hours without compromising adequate illuminance — daylighting-integrated lighting control systems increasingly take advantage of this to reduce energy consumption, using photosensors to automatically adjust artificial lighting output based on measured actual illuminance (from combined daylight and artificial sources) rather than assuming artificial lighting alone must meet the full target lux level at all times.

Summary: use Lux = Lumens ÷ Area for a quick average illuminance estimate, check the result against the recommended lux range for your specific space and task, remember this gives a spatial average rather than a point measurement, and use a lux meter for final verification on critical applications where actual measured performance matters more than a preliminary calculation.

Connecting lux calculation to energy-efficient lighting design: once a target lux level and room area are established, the required total lumen output follows directly (Lumens = Lux × Area, the same formula rearranged), which then drives fixture selection and quantity — modern LED fixtures offer considerably more lumens per watt than older lighting technologies, meaning the same target lux level can often be achieved with substantially less energy consumption than an older-technology installation would require. This relationship between target illuminance, fixture efficacy (lumens per watt), and total energy consumption is central to energy-efficient lighting retrofit and design decisions, building directly on the basic lux calculation covered here.

Task-specific versus general (ambient) lighting strategy: rather than lighting an entire space uniformly to the highest lux level any task within it requires, many efficient lighting designs use a layered approach — general ambient lighting at a moderate level covering the whole space, supplemented by task-specific lighting (a desk lamp, a focused inspection light) providing the additional illuminance needed only where and when the more demanding visual task actually occurs. This approach can reduce overall energy consumption compared to uniformly lighting an entire space to the highest standard any single task within it requires, while still providing adequate illuminance exactly where it's needed.

Lux calculation as a starting point for lighting retrofit projects: when evaluating whether an existing space's lighting is adequate, or planning an LED retrofit of older fixtures, measuring or calculating current illuminance against the recommended range for that space's actual use gives a clear, quantified basis for the retrofit decision — rather than replacing fixtures based on age or general impression alone, a lux-based assessment identifies specifically whether a space is under-lit, adequately lit, or over-lit relative to its actual functional requirements, informing a more precisely targeted (and often more cost-effective) retrofit scope.

Working with a lighting designer or using dedicated design software: for anything beyond a rough preliminary estimate — a genuinely new lighting design, a space with unusual geometry, or an application with strict regulatory illuminance requirements — dedicated lighting design software (using detailed photometric fixture data, room surface reflectance values, and proper point-by-point or zonal calculation methods) gives considerably more accurate and actionable results than this simplified average calculation. This calculator remains a genuinely useful quick-check and educational tool for understanding the fundamental lumens-illuminance-area relationship that underlies all of that more detailed design work.

Worked Example

12,000 lumens over 30 m²: Illuminance = 12,000 ÷ 30 = 400 lux (≈ 37 foot-candles) — suitable for general office work.

This calculator gives average illuminance across a defined area, assuming reasonably even light distribution — real installations have some variation across the illuminated area depending on luminaire placement, room reflectance, and fixture type. For a complete room lighting design (accounting for luminaire count, utilization factor, and maintenance factor), use a dedicated room lighting design calculation rather than this basic lumens-to-lux relationship alone. For point-source or spot-lighting scenarios, use the inverse square law instead of this area-average approach, and consider daylighting contribution for spaces with significant natural light.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Calculating or Interpreting Lux

1. Confusing lumens (total light output) with lux (illuminance on a surface). A fixture's lumen rating alone doesn't tell you the resulting lux without knowing the illuminated area — always divide by area to get a meaningful illuminance figure comparable to lux-based recommendations.

2. Using floor area when the relevant illuminated area is actually the working plane at a different level. Illuminance is typically specified at the working plane (commonly desk or task height, around 0.75-0.85m above floor), not necessarily identical to the room's total floor area, particularly for irregular room shapes or partial-area task lighting.

3. Assuming this average calculation represents illuminance at every point in the room equally. Real illuminance varies across a space depending on fixture placement — this calculation gives a useful average estimate, not a guarantee of uniform lux everywhere, and areas far from any fixture will read lower than this average.

4. Not accounting for light output degradation over the fixture's service life. Lamp lumen output typically decreases somewhat over its operating life, and dirt/dust accumulation on fixtures further reduces delivered light — a complete lighting design applies a maintenance factor to account for this, which this basic calculation doesn't include.

5. Assuming higher lux is always better regardless of the task. Excessive illuminance beyond what a task actually requires wastes energy and can cause glare or visual discomfort without improving task performance — target the appropriate recommended range for your specific space and task, not simply the maximum achievable.

6. Confusing lux with lumens when comparing different light fixtures or bulbs. A bulb's lumen rating (fixed, a property of the bulb) is different from the lux it will produce in your specific space (which depends on that lumen output divided by your specific room's area) — comparing bulbs by lumens alone doesn't tell you the resulting illuminance without factoring in your actual space.

7. Ignoring the inverse square law for point-source or spot-lighting calculations. This calculator's area-average approach doesn't apply to a single point source at a specific distance — use E = I÷d² for point-source illuminance calculations instead, which follows a fundamentally different (inverse-square, not simply additive) relationship.

8. Overlooking daylighting contribution when calculating total required artificial lighting. Spaces with significant natural light can achieve adequate illuminance from daylight alone during much of the day — sizing artificial lighting to meet full target lux at all times, ignoring daylight contribution, can lead to unnecessary energy consumption in well-daylit spaces.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for illuminance in lux? +

Illuminance (lux) = Total Luminous Flux (lumens) ÷ Illuminated Area (m²). Lux is literally defined as lumens per square meter, making this the most fundamental relationship in photometric lighting calculations.

What is the difference between lumens and lux? +

Lumens measure total light output from a source, independent of how that light is spread out. Lux measures illuminance — how much of that light actually falls on a given area, which depends on both total lumens and how spread out (over what area) that light is distributed. The same lumen output spread over a smaller area produces higher lux (brighter illuminance) than the identical lumen output spread over a larger area.

Why does the same light bulb produce different lux readings in different rooms? +

Lux depends on the area being illuminated, not just the light source's lumen output — the same bulb in a small room concentrates its lumens over less area, producing higher lux, while the identical bulb in a larger room spreads the same lumens over more area, producing lower lux. Room shape, ceiling height, wall/ceiling reflectance, and fixture placement also affect real-world lux distribution beyond this simplified average calculation.

What is a typical lux level for general office work? +

Around 300-500 lux is commonly recommended for general office and reading tasks, though specific tasks requiring finer visual detail (detailed inspection, technical drawing) may call for higher levels (500-1000 lux or more), while general circulation areas (corridors, storage) typically need considerably less (100-200 lux).

How is this basic lux calculation different from a full room lighting design? +

This calculator gives the fundamental lumens-to-lux relationship for a defined illuminated area, assuming all emitted lumens usefully reach that area — a complete room lighting design additionally accounts for utilization factor (how efficiently luminaires deliver light to the working plane, affected by room geometry and reflectance) and maintenance factor (accounting for light output degradation over time from dirt and lamp aging), both of which reduce actual delivered lux below this simplified calculation's result.

Does the color or type of light source affect lux measurement? +

Lux is a photometric quantity weighted by human eye sensitivity (which varies by wavelength/color), so different light sources with the identical radiometric power output can produce different lux readings depending on their spectral composition — lumens (and therefore lux) already account for this human-eye-weighting, which is why lumens/lux are the standard practical units for lighting design rather than raw optical power (watts of light).

Why do some spaces need much higher lux levels than others? +

Required lux level scales with how visually demanding the task performed in that space is — spaces requiring fine visual discrimination (surgery, detailed inspection, precision assembly) need considerably more light than spaces used mainly for circulation or general orientation (corridors, storage areas, parking), reflecting genuine differences in visual task difficulty, not arbitrary standards.

Can too much lux be a problem, not just too little? +

Yes — excessive illuminance can cause glare, visual discomfort, and unnecessary energy consumption without providing proportional benefit to visual task performance once adequate lux for the task is already achieved — lighting design generally targets an appropriate range for the specific task, not simply the maximum achievable lux level.

Should I measure actual lux with a meter, or rely only on calculation? +

For critical applications or final verification, a lux meter measurement at the actual working plane gives the most accurate, real-world confirmation — calculations (including this one) are valuable for planning and preliminary design, but real installations can differ from calculated values due to factors like fixture aging, dirt accumulation, room reflectance assumptions, and actual luminaire placement versus idealized assumptions.

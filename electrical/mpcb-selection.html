How it works

How MPCB Dial Setting Is Determined

A Motor Protection Circuit Breaker (MPCB) is purpose-built for motor circuits, combining two protection functions that would otherwise need separate devices: an adjustable thermal overload element (protecting against sustained overload during running) and a fixed instantaneous magnetic element (protecting against short-circuit faults). Its adjustable current dial is what makes correct setting — not just correct model selection — the critical step in getting an MPCB to actually protect the specific motor it's installed on.

Formula used: MPCB Dial Setting (A) = Motor Full Load Current × Service Factor. The motor's nameplate FLC is its rated full-load operating current; service factor (commonly 1.0, or 1.15 for many general-purpose motors designed with some built-in overload tolerance) tells you how far above that nameplate FLC the motor can be intentionally, continuously operated without exceeding its safe temperature limits.

Worked example: a motor with a nameplate FLC of 18 A and a 1.0 service factor (no built-in overload allowance). Dial Setting = 18 × 1.0 = 18 A — set the MPCB's adjustable dial directly to 18 A, selecting a specific MPCB model whose adjustable range brackets this value from the manufacturer's catalog. If the same motor instead had a 1.15 service factor, Dial Setting = 18 × 1.15 = 20.7 A, reflecting that this particular motor is designed to safely handle up to 115% of its nameplate rating continuously.

Why the dial setting should track FLC closely, not be padded upward: unlike fuse or MCB sizing for motor circuits (which use a large multiplier like 175-300% specifically because a fuse or MCB lacks an adjustable, motor-specific thermal characteristic), an MPCB's thermal overload element is purpose-built to distinguish normal starting inrush from genuine sustained overload — it's designed to ride through a brief starting surge at its rated setting without needing extra numerical headroom the way a simple fuse does. Setting the dial meaningfully above actual FLC "to avoid nuisance tripping" defeats this purpose-built precision and removes real overload protection margin, which is exactly the opposite of what an MPCB is chosen for in the first place.

Instantaneous (magnetic) trip — fixed, not adjustable, on most models: while the thermal overload element is adjustable via the dial, the instantaneous short-circuit trip threshold on most standard MPCB models is fixed at the factory, commonly in the range of 12-14 times the dial setting for Class 10 motor protection circuit breakers under IEC 60947-4-1. This fixed multiplier is calibrated to comfortably tolerate a motor's normal locked-rotor starting current (typically 5-8× FLC) with margin, while still responding quickly to a genuine short-circuit event, which by definition draws current well beyond even a heavy starting surge.

Star-delta starting and MPCB placement: where an MPCB sits in a star-delta starting circuit changes what current it actually experiences and therefore how it should be set. An MPCB placed in the main incoming line, ahead of the star/delta contactor arrangement, sees the motor's full line current throughout both starting and running, and should be set to the motor's normal full-load line current as usual. An MPCB placed specifically within the delta-winding branch circuits (a less common but sometimes-used arrangement) carries winding (phase) current rather than line current, which in a delta connection is the line current divided by √3 — about 0.58 times the nameplate line FLC — so the dial must be set on that reduced basis instead. Always confirm exactly where in the starting circuit topology an MPCB is positioned before finalizing its dial setting, since this is a genuinely easy point to get wrong without careful attention to the specific circuit diagram.

Class 10 vs Class 20 vs Class 30 overload tripping characteristics: MPCBs (and motor overload relays generally) are categorized by trip class, describing how long they'll tolerate a sustained overload before tripping — Class 10 (tripping within roughly 10 seconds at 7.2× setting) suits motors with typically fast starting; Class 20 and Class 30 allow progressively longer tolerance, suited to motors with longer, heavier starting duty (high-inertia loads that take longer to accelerate to full speed) that would otherwise nuisance-trip a faster-class device during a legitimately longer, but still normal, starting event. Selecting the correct trip class for your specific motor's starting duty is a separate consideration from the dial current setting calculated here, and getting it wrong (too fast a class for a slow-starting high-inertia load) is a common source of starting-related nuisance tripping even when the current dial setting itself is correct.

MPCB and contactor combination for remote/automatic switching: an MPCB alone provides protection but is typically a manually operated device — for motor circuits needing remote start/stop control (from a control system, a start/stop pushbutton station, or automatic sequencing), an MPCB is commonly paired with a separate contactor, with the MPCB handling protection (both thermal overload and short-circuit) and the contactor handling the actual repeated make/break switching duty that a protective device alone isn't designed for. Some integrated products combine both functions, but understanding this division of labor helps when specifying a complete motor starter assembly rather than a standalone protective device.

Coordination type between MPCB and contactor (Type 1 vs Type 2): when a genuine short circuit occurs downstream of an MPCB-and-contactor combination, the MPCB's fast instantaneous trip needs to protect the contactor from damage as well as the motor circuit itself — IEC 60947-4-1 defines coordination Type 1 (contactor may be damaged but does not create a safety hazard, may need replacement after a serious fault) and Type 2 (contactor and MPCB both remain fully serviceable after clearing even a maximum-rated fault, no replacement needed). Type 2 coordination is generally preferred where minimizing downtime and replacement cost after a fault matters, and achieving it requires using manufacturer-tested and certified MPCB-contactor combinations rather than assembling components independently and assuming compatibility.

Summary: use Dial Setting = Motor FLC × Service Factor, set the dial as close to actual FLC as practical rather than padding it upward, confirm MPCB placement relative to any star-delta or other special starting arrangement before finalizing the setting, select the correct trip class (10/20/30) for the motor's actual starting duty, and use manufacturer-tested MPCB-contactor combinations for reliable Type 2 coordination where minimizing post-fault downtime matters.

Re-checking settings after a motor replacement: if a motor is later replaced (even with a nominally "equivalent" unit), always re-verify the MPCB dial setting against the new motor's actual nameplate FLC rather than assuming it's identical to the original — different manufacturers, efficiency classes, or even production batches of a nominally similar motor can have somewhat different actual FLC ratings, and leaving an MPCB set for the original motor's FLC after a replacement can result in either nuisance tripping or inadequate protection for the new motor's actual characteristics.

Documenting the setting for future maintenance: recording the calculated dial setting, the motor FLC and service factor it was based on, and the date it was set (ideally on a label at the panel or in facility documentation) helps future maintenance staff quickly verify the setting is still correct, or identify when and why it might need updating — this is a small documentation habit that pays off significantly when troubleshooting an unexplained trip months or years later, especially in facilities with many similar motor circuits where settings could otherwise be easily confused between units.

A closing note on why this small dial adjustment deserves real attention: it's easy to treat setting an MPCB dial as a trivial, five-second step compared to the more involved work of selecting the right frame, breaking capacity, or coordination scheme — but an otherwise perfectly selected MPCB with an incorrectly set dial provides no better protection than the wrong device entirely. The calculation itself is simple, but getting the input right (actual nameplate FLC, correct service factor, correct understanding of circuit placement) and actually performing the adjustment during commissioning, not just specifying it on paper, is what turns a correctly selected device into actual, working motor protection.

Motor FLC=18A, service factor 1.0: Dial Setting = 18 × 1.0 = 18 A. Typical instantaneous trip (12-14×): 216-252 A.

Protection Zones

Reading an MPCB Trip Curve

Every number this calculator produces maps onto one region of the breaker's time–current characteristic. Reading that characteristic once makes the difference between guessing at a nuisance trip and diagnosing it, because the shape of the curve tells you which element operated and therefore what actually went wrong.

The sloping blue line is the thermal element. It is inverse-time: the further current rises above the dial setting, the faster it operates. Moving the dial slides this whole curve left or right, which is precisely why the dial value has to match the motor rather than the panel designer's comfort level.

The amber band is where a direct-on-line start lives — commonly five to eight times full-load current for a second or two. The curve passes above that band, so a healthy start finishes long before the thermal element accumulates enough heat to operate. That clearance is the whole design intent, and it is why padding the dial upward buys nothing but lost protection.

The red vertical line is the instantaneous magnetic element. On most standard models it is factory-fixed, typically around twelve to fourteen times the dial setting, and it clears a genuine short circuit in milliseconds. A fault current large enough to reach that line is far beyond anything a starting motor draws, which is how one device distinguishes the two events without any setting from you.

Diagnosing from the curve: a trip after several seconds of running, with the thermal indicator showing, points at the sloping region — genuine overload, phase imbalance, a stalled driven machine, or a dial set below actual FLC. An instant trip at the moment of switching points at the vertical region — a short circuit, a winding or cable fault, or a magnetic threshold too low for that motor's inrush. The two failure modes look identical from the panel door but sit at opposite ends of this graph.

Model Selection

Choosing the Right MPCB Adjustment Range

Calculating the dial setting is only half the job. An MPCB is ordered as a specific model covering a specific adjustable band, so the second decision is which band to buy — and a calculated setting that technically falls inside a band is not the same as one that sits comfortably within it.

Take the worked example above: a motor at 18 A with unity service factor needs a dial setting of 18 A. Several catalogue bands touch that number, but they are not equally suitable.

A band whose minimum sits above the required setting is unusable, however close it looks — the dial simply cannot be turned down far enough, so the motor would be protected at a current it is not rated to carry. A band whose maximum sits far above leaves the pointer near the bottom stop, where the graduations are coarsest and small adjustments are least repeatable.

The band ending exactly at the required value is the more interesting case. It is not wrong, and plenty of installations run that way. But it leaves nothing in reserve: no room to trim the setting upward after commissioning measurements, and no room at all if the motor is later swapped for a nominally equivalent unit whose nameplate reads a little higher. Where two bands both contain the calculated value, the one that brackets it is the safer order.

Why nameplate current beats a power rating every time

Two motors sharing a kilowatt or horsepower rating can draw meaningfully different currents, because current depends on rated voltage, efficiency class, power factor, pole count and the manufacturer's own design choices. A 7.5 kW motor does not have one current; it has a different one at 380 V, 400 V and 415 V, and a higher-efficiency model will typically draw less than an older one of the same output. Power-to-current tables are a reasonable way to narrow down which frame to quote, and nothing more.

Where the nameplate genuinely is not available yet — specifying a panel before the motor is delivered, for instance — a three-phase motor’s line current can be approximated from its output rating using I ≈ P ÷ (√3 × V × η × PF), with P in watts, V the line-to-line voltage, η the efficiency as a decimal and PF the power factor. Treat that figure as provisional and re-check the dial against the real nameplate at commissioning, because the estimate inherits the error of every assumed term in it.

One more nameplate detail is easy to miss: where a motor lists two voltages and two currents, the figure that matters is the one matching the supply voltage and the winding connection actually used. Wiring for the other connection and setting the dial from the wrong column is a quiet way to end up with protection that is out by a factor of √3.

Circuit Topology

MPCB Selection by Starting Method

The dial setting depends on what the breaker actually carries, and that depends on where it sits. The same motor, the same nameplate, and the same MPCB can require two different settings purely because of the position chosen in the starter.

Starting Method Dial Setting Basis What to Watch
Direct-on-lineNameplate line FLC × service factorHighest inrush of any method; confirm the trip class suits the acceleration time
Star-delta, MPCB in main lineNameplate line FLC × service factorBreaker sees line current in both star and delta stages
Star-delta, MPCB inside the delta branchAbout 0.58 × line FLC (line FLC ÷ √3)Easy to set from the wrong column; verify against the actual starter schematic
Soft starter, MPCB upstreamNameplate line FLC, then check the soft starter maker's coordination tableReduced inrush but longer ramp; extended acceleration still heats the motor
Variable frequency drive, MPCB on the drive inputDrive input current rating, not the motor FLCProtects the feeder and serves as an isolating device — it gives the motor no overload protection

The drive case is the one most often got wrong. An MPCB ahead of a VFD sees the rectifier's input current, which is shaped by the drive rather than by the motor, so setting it to motor FLC is meaningless. Overload protection for a drive-fed motor comes from the drive's own electronic thermal model, configured with the motor's nameplate data. Switching a contactor or breaker between a running drive and its motor is a separate hazard altogether, and should only be done in an arrangement both manufacturers explicitly support.

Soft starters deserve a note too. Limiting starting current does not limit starting heat: a motor ramped gently over twenty seconds can absorb more thermal energy than the same motor slammed across the line for three. If starting duty is heavy or frequent, the trip class matters more than the current limit does.

If it trips during starting, do not turn the dial up

Raising the setting is the fastest way to make the symptom disappear and the underlying fault permanent. Before touching the dial, work through the real candidates: is the trip class matched to how long this machine genuinely takes to reach speed; is the supply sagging under inrush; is the driven equipment stiff, jammed or loaded at the moment of start; are the three phases balanced; how many starts per hour is the motor seeing; and is the magnetic threshold on this particular model simply too low for this motor's inrush. A dial set correctly and tripping repeatedly is telling you something true.

Fault Withstand

Breaking Capacity: The Check That Current Range Does Not Cover

A breaker can be perfectly matched to a motor's running current and still be the wrong device, because current range says nothing about what happens during a fault. The second requirement is that the MPCB can interrupt the prospective short-circuit current available at the point where it is installed — a figure set by the supply transformer's rating and impedance, the cable run between transformer and panel, and the wider system configuration, not by the motor at all.

Two ratings appear on the datasheet and they are not interchangeable:

Rating Meaning Practical Reading
IcuUltimate breaking capacityThe device clears the fault safely, but may not be fit for further service afterwards
IcsService breaking capacityThe device clears the fault and remains serviceable; often quoted as a percentage of Icu

Both are declared at a stated operational voltage, and both fall as that voltage rises — a figure quoted at 400 V does not carry over to a 480 V or 690 V installation. Check the rating against the voltage you are actually running.

Where the prospective fault current at the panel exceeds what the MPCB can interrupt on its own, the usual answer is backup protection: an upstream fuse or breaker, of a type the manufacturer has tested in that combination, that takes the peak energy out of the fault before the MPCB has to. This is a tested pairing, not an assembled one — substituting a fuse of the same nominal rating from a different family is not the same device.

The related decision is coordination class with the contactor. Type 1 accepts that the contactor may be damaged by a full-rated fault, provided nothing hazardous escapes the enclosure; the starter is expected to be repaired or replaced afterwards. Type 2 requires that both devices remain fit for continued service, with no more than light contact welding that can be separated. Type 2 is the sensible default wherever a fault means production downtime, and it can only be claimed from the manufacturer's tested combination tables — a pairing of two components that each look adequate in isolation gives no coordination class at all.

Comparison

MPCB vs Separate Overload Relay vs Fuse-Based Motor Protection

Protection Scheme Devices Needed Notes
MPCB1 deviceCombined overload + short-circuit, adjustable dial, common with a contactor for remote switching
MCCB/MCB + separate overload relay2 devicesBreaker for short-circuit, relay for overload; more flexible overload curve options in some cases

MPCBs have become increasingly popular specifically because they consolidate what used to require two or three separate devices into one, simplifying panel design, reducing wiring, and often reducing the number of spare parts a facility needs to stock. The trade-off is typically a somewhat higher per-device cost than a basic fuse, offset by the savings in panel space, wiring labor, and simplified coordination between fewer devices — for many modern motor circuit designs, particularly in compact panel layouts, this trade-off favors the MPCB approach.

When retrofitting or expanding an existing panel, matching the incumbent protection scheme already used throughout the facility often outweighs a theoretically "better" alternative for a single new circuit, simply for consistency in spare parts, maintenance familiarity, and staff training — a genuinely new panel design has more freedom to choose the scheme best suited to that specific project's requirements from the outset.

International Practice

MPCB Selection Across Standards and Regions

The physics of motor overload protection is the same everywhere, but the vocabulary, the governing standard and — importantly — the sizing basis are not. A setting that is correct under one framework can be non-compliant under another, so it is worth knowing which one applies before turning the dial.

The device has different names in different markets

"MPCB" is IEC-world shorthand. The same device is sold as a manual motor starter, a manual motor protector or a manual motor controller depending on the manufacturer and the market, and in North America a version tested and marked for use without a separate upstream device is described as a self-protected combination motor controller. Searching a supplier catalogue under only one of those terms is a common way to conclude a product does not exist when it plainly does.

The sizing multiplier is not universal

Under IEC practice the thermal element is normally set at the motor's rated nameplate current, with the service factor applied where the motor carries one above unity. The trip characteristic itself is built to absorb normal starting, so no further multiplier is added unless the manufacturer's documentation calls for one.

Where the US National Electrical Code applies, separate motor overload protection for a continuous-duty motor above 1 hp is instead sized as a percentage of the motor's nameplate full-load amperes: commonly 125% where the motor has a marked service factor of 1.15 or greater or a marked temperature rise of 40°C or less, and 115% otherwise, with provisions for increasing the value where the motor will not start or run without tripping. Note also that the code distinguishes between the nameplate amperes used for overload sizing and the table values used for conductor and short-circuit device sizing — they are deliberately different numbers for different jobs.

This calculator multiplies FLC by whatever factor you enter, so it serves both conventions: enter the motor's actual service factor for IEC-style practice, or enter 1.25 or 1.15 to follow the NEC percentage basis. Whichever you use, the edition of the code or standard in force in your jurisdiction governs, and the manufacturer's instructions for the specific device take precedence over any general rule.

Supply voltage changes the current, not the method

The same motor output rating produces a different full-load current on every common low-voltage system, which is exactly why the nameplate — matched to the actual supply — is the only safe input to this calculation.

Frequency deserves one clarification, because it is a frequent point of confusion: 50 Hz and 60 Hz do not change the dial-setting arithmetic. They do change the motor — synchronous speed, and therefore the current a given driven load demands — which is why a motor moved between the two systems needs its nameplate re-read rather than its old setting re-used. A device rated for one frequency should also be confirmed as rated for the other before being used across markets.

Ambient temperature is the last regional variable worth naming. Thermal elements are calibrated at a reference ambient, and a panel in a hot plant room or an un-air-conditioned enclosure will run its thermal element warmer than the calibration assumed, tripping earlier than the dial suggests. Where the installation is genuinely hot, or unusually cold, check whether the manufacturer publishes a temperature compensation table or an ambient-compensated variant for that model.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes When Selecting an MPCB

1. Setting the dial above actual motor FLC to avoid nuisance tripping. This directly removes real overload protection margin — an MPCB's thermal element is purpose-built to handle normal starting without needing this padding; if nuisance tripping occurs at a correctly-set dial, investigate the actual cause rather than simply setting the dial higher.

2. Forgetting to apply service factor when the motor has one greater than 1.0. A motor with a 1.15 service factor is designed to run continuously up to 115% of nameplate FLC — setting the dial to bare FLC without this adjustment can cause nuisance tripping under conditions the motor is actually rated to handle.

3. Using a generic reference FLC instead of the specific motor's actual nameplate value. Generic horsepower-to-FLC tables give approximate values that can differ meaningfully from a specific motor's actual tested and rated FLC — always use the real nameplate figure for the motor actually being installed.

4. Not confirming MPCB placement relative to star-delta starting contactors before setting the dial. An MPCB in the main line versus one in the delta-winding branch circuits sees different currents — using main-line FLC for a branch-circuit-positioned MPCB (or vice versa) gives an incorrect setting.

5. Assuming the instantaneous trip threshold is adjustable when it typically isn't. On most standard MPCB models, only the thermal overload dial is field-adjustable; the instantaneous magnetic trip is fixed at manufacture — don't assume you can fine-tune both independently unless your specific model explicitly offers an adjustable instantaneous setting.

6. Checking the current range but never the breaking capacity. A correctly set dial says nothing about whether the device can interrupt the fault current available at that panel — verify Icu and Ics at the actual operational voltage, and add tested backup protection where the prospective fault current exceeds them.

7. Setting an MPCB on a VFD input to the motor's full load current. A breaker ahead of a drive carries the drive's input current and protects the feeder; motor overload protection on a drive-fed motor comes from the drive's own thermal model, configured with the motor nameplate data.

8. Reusing the old setting after a motor is replaced. A nominally equivalent replacement can have a different nameplate current — re-read the nameplate and re-set the dial rather than assuming the previous value still applies.

9. Selecting an MPCB model whose adjustable range doesn't comfortably bracket the calculated setting. A calculated setting sitting right at the very edge of a model's minimum or maximum range leaves little room for fine adjustment or for a slightly different motor being substituted later — where practical, select a model whose range comfortably includes the calculated setting with some margin on either side.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an MPCB and how is it different from a standard MCB or MCCB? +

An MPCB (Motor Protection Circuit Breaker) combines adjustable thermal overload protection and short-circuit (magnetic) protection in a single device specifically designed for motor circuits, with an adjustable current dial that lets one physical device cover a range of motor full load currents — unlike a standard MCB or MCCB, which typically has a fixed current rating and isn't designed with motor-specific thermal overload characteristics built in.

How do I set an MPCB's dial for my motor? +

Set the dial to the motor's rated full load current (FLC) as shown on its nameplate, multiplied by the motor's service factor if it has one greater than 1.0 — this dial setting configures the thermal overload trip point specifically for that motor's actual full load operating current.

What is motor service factor and why does it affect the dial setting? +

Service factor is a multiplier (commonly 1.0 or 1.15 for many general-purpose motors) indicating how much above its nameplate rated load a motor can safely handle continuously without exceeding its insulation temperature limits — a motor with a 1.15 service factor can be intentionally operated up to 115% of its nameplate FLC, so the MPCB dial setting should be adjusted upward to match, rather than tripping the motor at its base FLC when it's actually designed to handle more.

Does the MPCB dial setting change for star-delta starting? +

It depends on where the MPCB is installed in the starting circuit — if placed in the main incoming line ahead of the star-delta contactors, it sees full line current and should be set to the motor's full-load line current as usual. If placed in the delta-winding branch circuits specifically, it sees a different (lower, by a factor of √3) current than the main line current, requiring a correspondingly adjusted setting — always confirm exactly where in the starting circuit the MPCB sits before finalizing its setting.

What is the typical instantaneous (short-circuit) trip threshold for an MPCB? +

Commonly in the range of roughly 12-14 times the dial setting for standard Class 10 motor protection circuit breakers per IEC 60947-4-1, though this varies by specific manufacturer and product — this instantaneous threshold is fixed (not adjustable) on most MPCB models, unlike the thermal overload setting, and is calibrated to tolerate normal motor starting inrush while still responding quickly to a genuine short circuit.

Can one MPCB model cover multiple different motor sizes? +

Yes, within its specific adjustable current range — a single MPCB model can be dial-set anywhere within its published minimum-to-maximum current range, so the same physical device (and stocked spare part) can protect several different motor sizes whose FLC falls within that range, simply by adjusting the dial setting for each specific installation, which is a genuine inventory and standardization advantage over fixed-rating protective devices.

Does an MPCB eliminate the need for a separate motor overload relay? +

Yes, for the overload protection function specifically — an MPCB's whole design purpose is combining short-circuit and thermal overload protection in one device, unlike a standard MCCB or fuse which typically needs a separate overload relay for adequate motor protection. This combination is one of the main practical advantages of MPCBs in motor circuit design, simplifying the panel and reducing the number of separate protective devices needed.

Should I use the motor's FLC from its nameplate or from a general reference table? +

Always use the specific motor's actual nameplate FLC, not a generic reference table value for that motor's approximate horsepower/kW rating — nameplate FLC reflects the actual, specific motor's tested characteristics and can differ meaningfully from generic table estimates, and using the wrong figure can result in an incorrectly set MPCB that either nuisance-trips or fails to protect the motor adequately.

What happens if the MPCB dial is set too high or too low for the actual motor? +

Setting it too low causes nuisance tripping under normal load or even during a slightly longer-than-typical start, while setting it too high fails to protect the motor from a genuine sustained overload condition that could overheat and damage the windings — the dial should be set as close as practical to the motor's actual FLC (adjusted for service factor), not deliberately padded high to avoid nuisance tripping, since that padding directly removes real overload protection margin.

Is an MPCB sized the same way under IEC practice and the US National Electrical Code? +

Not quite. Under IEC practice the thermal element is normally set at the motor's rated nameplate current, with the service factor applied where the motor has one above unity, because the trip characteristic is already built to ride through normal starting. Where the NEC applies, separate overload protection for a continuous-duty motor above 1 hp is instead sized as a percentage of nameplate full-load amperes — commonly 125% where the motor is marked with a service factor of 1.15 or greater or a temperature rise of 40°C or less, and 115% otherwise. This calculator multiplies FLC by whatever factor you enter, so enter the actual service factor for IEC-style practice or 1.25 / 1.15 to follow the NEC basis. The edition in force in your jurisdiction and the device manufacturer's instructions always govern.

What breaking capacity does my MPCB need, and what is the difference between Icu and Ics? +

The device must be able to interrupt the prospective short-circuit current available where it is installed, which depends on the supply transformer rating and impedance, the cable run and the system configuration — not on the motor. Icu is the ultimate breaking capacity: the device clears the fault safely but may not be fit for further service. Ics is the service breaking capacity: it clears the fault and stays serviceable, and is often quoted as a percentage of Icu. Both are declared at a stated operational voltage and fall as voltage rises, so a figure quoted at 400 V does not carry over to 480 V or 690 V. Where the available fault current exceeds these ratings, manufacturer-tested backup protection is required.

Can I put an MPCB between a VFD and the motor, or set one on the drive input to the motor FLC? +

Neither, as a general rule. An MPCB on the drive input carries the drive's input current and protects the feeder while serving as an isolating device — it should be selected for that duty, not set to the motor's full-load current, and it does not give the motor overload protection. Overload protection for a drive-fed motor comes from the drive's own electronic thermal model, configured with the motor nameplate data. Switching a breaker or contactor between a running drive and its motor is a separate hazard and should only be done in an arrangement both manufacturers explicitly support.

How do I choose the MPCB adjustment range, not just the dial value? +

Pick a catalogue band that contains the calculated setting with room on both sides. A band whose minimum sits above the required value cannot be turned down far enough and is unusable. A band whose maximum sits far above leaves the pointer near the bottom stop where adjustment is least repeatable. A band that ends exactly at the required value works but leaves no reserve for a commissioning adjustment or for a replacement motor with a slightly higher nameplate current — where two bands both contain the value, order the one that brackets it.
